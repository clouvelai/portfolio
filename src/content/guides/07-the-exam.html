The exam: how we know a model is good, and when the number is lying

Every claim that one model beats another comes down to a number from an eval. This page takes that number apart: where it comes from, how much it wobbles, who decides what counts as right, and the ways it misleads. Start with the oldest trap. Two models, one benchmark. Run it.

00 · PLAIN WORDS

Every term, in one breath

Read this first or come back to it. Every dotted word on the page links here. Statistics terms get a picture; benchmark names are at the end.

Eval, benchmark
An eval is any repeatable procedure that turns a model into a number. A benchmark is a published, fixed eval that many people run so their numbers can be compared.A driving test versus a national driving test with published routes.
Item, test set
One question with its reference answer is an item. The test set is all the items. Its size, n, sets how precise the final number can be.Each question on the exam paper, and the paper.
Metric, accuracy
The rule that turns per-item grades into one number. Accuracy is the share of items graded correct. Others: F1, mean rubric score, win rate, pass@k.How the marks are added up.
Prompt template, few-shot
The exact wrapping around each question: instructions, answer format, and worked examples ("shots"). Changing only the template can move a score by many points, so it is part of the eval, not a detail.The wording of the exam instructions. "Circle one letter" and "explain your answer" are different exams.
Answer extraction
Code that pulls the final answer out of the model's text: the first letter, the text after "The answer is", the last number. When it fails, a right answer is scored wrong.The examiner who only reads the box at the bottom of the page.
Grader, verifier
Whatever decides if an extracted answer is right: exact string match, a maths equivalence check, unit tests that run the model's code, a judge model, or a person. A verifier is a grader that is a program.The answer key, the calculator, the test suite or the teacher.
LLM-as-judge, rubric
Using a language model to grade another model's answers, usually against a rubric: a list of criteria with points. Cheap and flexible; has its own biases and must itself be measured against human labels.A teaching assistant who marks fast and mostly agrees with the professor.
Pairwise comparison, win rate
Show two answers to the same prompt and ask which is better. Win rate is the share of comparisons a model wins. Easier for people than absolute scores, and the basis of arenas and preference training.An eye test: "better with lens one, or lens two?"
Elo, Bradley–Terry
Models that turn many pairwise wins into one rating per player, so that the rating gap predicts the win probability. 100 Elo points ≈ 64% expected wins. Arenas fit Bradley–Terry to millions of votes.Chess ratings for chatbots.
Standard error SE
How much a score would move if you reran the eval on a fresh set of questions of the same kind. For accuracy p on n items, SE = √(p(1−p)/n). It shrinks only with the square root of n: four times the questions, half the error.The wobble in a bathroom scale. Weigh yourself four times to halve it.
Confidence interval ± 1.96 SE
The score plus or minus about two standard errors. If you repeated the whole eval many times, 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true score. Two models whose intervals overlap heavily have not been separated."70%, give or take three" instead of "70%".
Paired comparison
Comparing two models on the same items and analysing the per-item differences. Because both models find the same questions easy or hard, the shared difficulty cancels and the difference is measured far more precisely than either score alone.Weighing two parcels on the same scale, one after the other, instead of on two different scales.
Power, minimum detectable difference
Power is the chance an eval detects a real difference of a given size. Turned around: with n items, the smallest gap you can reliably detect. Plan n from the gap you care about, not the other way round.The size of print an eye chart can still read at this distance.
Clustered error
When items come in groups (ten questions on one passage, many tests from one repository), they are not independent, and the honest standard error is larger than the formula above. Add up the errors within each cluster first, so a group counts as one piece of evidence.Ten opinions from one family are not ten independent opinions.
Run-to-run variance, temperature, seed
A model sampled at temperature above zero answers differently each time, so the same eval on the same items gives different scores. Reported numbers should say how many samples per item and average them.Asking the same student the same question on two different mornings.
pass@k
The chance that at least one of k attempts succeeds. Measures what a model can do if you can check its work and keep the best attempt. Estimated without bias from n samples with c correct as 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k), where C(n, k) counts the ways to pick k of n.Penalty shootout with k tries: you only need to score once.
pass^k
The chance that all k attempts succeed: how reliably the model does the task every time. Falls as k grows unless the model always succeeds. The number that matters for an agent running unattended.Score every penalty, every match, all season.
Contamination, leakage
Test items, or their answers, appearing in training data. The model then scores by remembering, not by solving. Hard to avoid because benchmarks are published on the web that training data is crawled from.Last year's exam paper circulating with the answers written in.
Canary string, held-out set
A canary is a unique random string printed in benchmark files so that crawlers can filter them out and anyone can check whether a model has seen them. A held-out (private) set is never published at all, so it cannot leak through the web.A dye pack in a bag of cash. Or keeping the real exam in a safe.
Perplexity, bits per character
How surprised a model is by a piece of text: the average negative log-probability per token or character. A model that has memorised a text is far less surprised by it than by a paraphrase of it, which is one way to detect contamination.Reading a page you have read before goes faster than reading the same story retold.
n-gram model
The simplest language model: it predicts the next character or word from the few before it, using counts from its training text. Too small to be clever, which makes memorisation easy to see.Autocomplete that has only ever read one book.
Saturation, headroom
A benchmark is saturated when top models score near its ceiling, which is often below 100% because some items are wrong or ambiguous. After that, differences between models are mostly noise and label errors.A ruler that stops at 30 cm, held up to a tall person.
Goodhart's law
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Optimising a benchmark, through training, prompt tuning or picking the best of many tries, raises the number faster than the ability it was meant to track.Teaching to the test.
Winner's curse, test-set overfitting
If you try many variants and keep the one with the best score on the same test, its score is biased upward: you selected for lucky noise. It will score lower on fresh questions. Every leaderboard and every hill-climbing team is exposed.The fund that beat the market last year, chosen out of a thousand funds.
Style control
Adjusting arena ratings for features voters reward that are not quality, mainly answer length and markdown formatting, by adding them to the rating model as separate terms.Judging a cake contest with the frosting scraped off.
Precision, recall, false pass
Treat a grader as a classifier against true labels. Precision: of the answers it passed, how many were right. Recall (also called sensitivity): of the right answers, how many it passed. Specificity: of the wrong answers, how many it failed. A false pass inflates scores; a false fail deflates them.A metal detector that beeps at keys (false pass) or misses a knife (false fail).
Agreement, Cohen's κ
How often two graders agree, corrected for how often they would agree by chance. κ = 1 is perfect, 0 is chance. Raw agreement looks good on easy sets where everything passes; κ does not.Two weather forecasters both saying "sunny" in the desert are not impressive.
Position, length and self-preference bias
Judge models tend to prefer the first answer shown, the longer answer, and answers written in their own style. Fixes: swap the order and average, control for length, use a judge from another family.A wine taster who always likes the first glass poured.
Agentic eval, trajectory
An eval where the model works for many steps with tools (a shell, a browser, an editor) to finish a task, graded on the end state: tests pass, file correct, ticket resolved. The trajectory is the full record of its steps.A practical exam in a workshop, not a written one.
Scaffold, harness, elicitation
The scaffold is the program around the model that gives it tools, memory and retries. The harness runs the eval. Elicitation is the effort spent getting the most capability out: better prompts, scaffolds, more attempts. Weak elicitation under-measures a model.The same athlete with running shoes or in flip-flops.
Time horizon
METR's measure of agent capability: the length of task, in the time a skilled human takes, that the model finishes with 50% (or 80%) success. Turns very different tasks into one scale measured in minutes or hours.Not "how hard a question" but "how long a job".
Dangerous-capability eval
An eval that asks whether a model could give meaningful help with serious harm (biological or chemical weapons, cyberattacks, escaping control). Results trigger safeguards under labs' published safety policies.A stress test on a bridge before anyone drives on it.
Capability versus propensity
Capability: can the model do X when pushed? Propensity: does it tend to do X when nobody asks? Different evals; a model can be capable of deception and rarely deceive, or the reverse.Whether someone can pick a lock, and whether they do.
Eval awareness, sandbagging
Eval awareness: a model recognising it is being tested, sometimes saying so in its reasoning. Sandbagging: performing worse on purpose in an eval. Both mean the test may not show how the model behaves in use.A driver who is perfect when the examiner is in the car.
Golden set, regression eval
A golden set is a small, carefully labelled set of your own real cases. A regression eval reruns it on every change (prompt, model, code) to catch things that got worse.The smoke alarm you test before you move in.
Offline versus online eval
Offline: a fixed test set run before shipping. Online: measuring real traffic after shipping, through A/B tests, user feedback and sampled transcripts. Offline catches regressions; only online tells you if users are better off.The flight simulator and the flight log.

Benchmarks and lab terms named on this page

MMLU 2020
57 subjects of four-choice questions, from high-school to professional level, about 14,000 test items. The standard knowledge test of 2021–2024; now saturated near 90%.
GPQA Diamond 2023
198 graduate-level biology, physics and chemistry questions written to be "Google-proof": PhD experts score about 65%, skilled non-experts with web access about 34%.
GSM8K, MATH, MATH-500
Grade-school word problems (1,319 test items) and competition maths (5,000 test items; MATH-500 is a 500-item subset). Graded by matching the final number or expression.
HumanEval 2021
164 Python functions to write from a docstring, graded by unit tests. Introduced the pass@k metric.
SWE-bench, Verified, Pro
Real GitHub issues from Python projects: the agent must change the repository so hidden tests pass. Verified (2024) is 500 tasks checked by engineers; OpenAI stopped reporting it in February 2026 over flawed tests and contamination. Pro is a harder, partly private successor.
Humanity's Last Exam HLE · 2025
2,500 expert-written questions across fields, kept only if frontier models failed them at the time. Launched with top scores under 10%.
ARC-AGI
Grid puzzles that are easy for people and need a new rule inferred from a few examples. Version 1 (2019) fell to OpenAI's o3 in December 2024; version 2 (2025) is harder.
τ-bench 2024
Simulated customer-service tasks (retail, airline) where an agent talks to a simulated user and calls tools. Introduced pass^k.
LMArena formerly Chatbot Arena · 2023
A public site where people chat with two anonymous models side by side and vote. Ratings come from millions of votes through Bradley–Terry.
METR
A non-profit that evaluates frontier models for autonomous capability; publishes the time-horizon measurements in chapter 09.

01 · ANATOMY OF A SCORE

A score is a pipeline, and every stage is a choice

"Model X scores 87% on MMLU" sounds like a fact about the model. It is a fact about the model plus a prompt template, a sampling setting, an answer extractor, a grader and a way of averaging. Two labs running "the same benchmark" often differ on three of those. Sclar et al. (2023) moved one open model's accuracy by up to 76 points by changing only spacing, separators and capitalisation in the template.

02 · ERROR BARS

Every score is a sample. Report the wobble.

A benchmark is a sample of questions from a much larger space of questions like them. Rerun with a different sample and the score moves. The standard error says by how much: for 70% on 500 items it is 2 points, so the 95% interval is about ±4. Most leaderboards print three significant figures and no interval, which is how a 1-point lead on 500 questions becomes a headline.

The good news is pairing. Two models answer the same questions, and questions that are hard for one tend to be hard for the other. Compare them item by item and that shared difficulty cancels, so the gap between them is known far more precisely than two separate error bars suggest. Miller (2024) recommends exactly this, plus clustered errors when items share a passage.

Top: where model A's and B's scores would land across many reruns (95% of the area is inside the shaded band). Bottom: the paired gap B − A, with zero marked. ρ is the item-level correlation between the two models' right/wrong answers; for models of the same family it is often 0.5 or more. The "n for 80% power" tile is how many questions you need to detect this gap four times out of five.

03 · WHO GRADES

The grader is a model of "correct", and it can be wrong

Exact string match is cheap, fast and wrong whenever a right answer is written differently. A regular expression is a little smarter and wrong in new ways. A maths verifier understands that 14/24 is 7/12 but not "seven twelfths". Unit tests check code by running it, which is why code benchmarks were the first to be trusted, and why a bad test suite misgrades: weak tests let wrong code pass, over-strict ones fail right code (OpenAI audited 138 tasks that models kept failing, about a quarter of the set, and found at least 59.4% of those SWE-bench Verified tasks had tests that reject correct fixes). Further right on the spectrum, judges and people cost more and agree less.

04 · JUDGING THE JUDGE

A judge model is an instrument. Calibrate it before you read it.

For open-ended answers (a support reply, a code review, a summary) there is no regex. Teams use a strong model with a rubric as the grader. Zheng et al. (2023) found GPT-4 as a judge agreed with human experts over 80% of the time, about as often as the experts agreed with each other. It is also biased: toward the first answer shown, toward longer answers, and toward its own style.

The fix is the same as for any instrument: label a few hundred items by hand, measure the judge's precision and recall and κ against them, pick a threshold, and correct the headline rate for the judge's known error. Below, 400 answers with true labels. Move the threshold, make the judge sharper or blunter, and make the model under test verbose.

A simulation: 400 answers, each with a true label and a judge score between 0 and 1. Dots above the axis are truly good answers, dots below truly bad. The judge passes everything right of the threshold. "Corrected rate" applies the standard misclassification fix, (judged rate + specificity − 1) / (sensitivity + specificity − 1), with sensitivity (recall) and specificity measured on a separate hand-labelled sample of 300 answers. Ticking "verbose" adds the length bias: every score shifts up, true quality does not.

05 · ONCE OR EVERY TIME

pass@k asks "can it ever?" pass^k asks "will it always?"

Sample a model k times on the same task. pass@k is the chance at least one attempt works: the right number when something checks the answers and keeps the good one, like a code generator with tests. pass^k is the chance every attempt works: the right number for an agent that runs unattended a thousand times a day. Two models with the same pass@1 can be wildly different on both. τ-bench introduced pass^k after finding that its best agent, GPT-4o, succeeded on fewer than half of all tasks, and on retail tasks fewer than a quarter succeeded eight times in a row.

Estimating pass@k from samples

Both models score exactly 60% at k = 1 across 20 tasks. Switch to "compare both": the steady model wins pass@k, the spiky one wins pass^k, because it reliably solves its easy half. The steady model's tasks all sit near 60%; the spiky model aces half and mostly fails the rest. Lower box: Chen et al.'s (2021) unbiased estimator against the naive plug-in 1 − (1 − c/n)ᵏ, which is biased low: it understates pass@k when samples are few. Sample more than k times per task and use the unbiased one.

06 · CONTAMINATION

If the test was in the training data, the score measures memory

Benchmarks are published on the web; training data is crawled from the web. Questions and answers leak through GitHub copies, blog posts and forum threads. Contamination is hard to see from the score alone, so labs and researchers look for its fingerprints: n-gram overlap between test and training text, canary strings, a model completing a question it should never have seen, and a gap between how easily it reads the original and a paraphrase. When Scale wrote GSM1k, 1,250 fresh problems in the style of GSM8K, some model families dropped by up to 8 points. In February 2026 OpenAI said frontier models could reproduce the exact fixes for SWE-bench Verified tasks from little more than the task ID.

07 · ARENAS

Let people vote, then fit a rating

Static benchmarks ask fixed questions. LMArena lets anyone type a prompt, shows answers from two anonymous models, and asks which is better. A Bradley–Terry model turns millions of these pairwise votes into ratings. Real prompts, real preferences, nothing fixed to memorise. Two weaknesses. Voters reward length and formatting, so since August 2024 the arena also publishes style-controlled ratings that put answer length and markdown into the model as separate terms. And labs can test many private variants and publish only the best: the Leaderboard Illusion paper (Singh et al., 2025) reported Meta testing 27 before Llama 4.

      08 · GOODHART

      When the score is the target, the score stops measuring

      Older benchmarks lasted years; the newest fall within one to two years, and part of that is real progress. Part is Goodhart's law: every team tunes prompts, picks checkpoints and chooses scaffolds by looking at the same public test, and each choice made on the test borrows a little of its noise. The best of many variants is the luckiest of many variants, so its score overstates it: the winner's curse. Once a benchmark saturates, label errors set the ceiling (an estimated 6.5% of MMLU's questions are wrong) and the leaderboard ranks noise.

      A simulation. Every variant starts from a 70% model. Each is scored on the same public test (horizontal axis) and on 5,000 fresh questions nobody tuned on (vertical). The orange dot is the one you would ship: best on the public test. "True gain" is the simulation's ground truth; the fresh-question score is itself a sample, within about ±1.3 points of the true gain 19 times in 20. "Average over 500 searches" reruns the whole search to show the bias is systematic, not bad luck.

      09 · TIME HORIZONS

      Measure agents by how long a job they can finish

      Agentic benchmarks score a pass rate on tasks of mixed difficulty, which says little about what an agent can be trusted with. METR timed skilled people on software and research tasks from seconds to many hours long, ran each model on all of them, and fitted a curve of success against task length. The 50% time horizon is where that curve crosses one half. Across 2019 to 2025 it doubled about every seven months, and it has sped up: METR's January 2026 re-measurement on its updated task suite (Time Horizon 1.1, or TH1.1, 228 tasks) puts the doubling since 2023 at about 131 days, roughly four months.

      Read the error bars before the trend. Claude Opus 4.5's 320 minutes has a 95% interval of 170 to 729. Only 5 of the 31 tasks over eight hours have a measured human time. The 80% horizon is several times shorter than the 50% one. And the suite is mostly software, with few tasks the newest models still fail.

      Real numbers from METR's January 2026 post: filled dots are the six models re-measured on the TH1.1 suite, with their 95% intervals; hollow dots are four models measured only on the older TH1 suite (METR's original 2025 task set), which reads somewhat lower. The trend line is a least-squares fit of log₂(minutes) against release date, computed in your browser; the dashed segment is extrapolation, not data.       10 · SAFETY EVALS

      Proving a model can't is harder than proving it can

      Labs' safety policies (Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework) tie safeguards to dangerous-capability evals in biology, cyber and autonomy. In May 2025 Anthropic turned on its ASL-3 protections (stricter security and deployment safeguards) for Claude Opus 4 because it could no longer rule out the biology threshold. These evals invert the usual logic. A product eval wants a fair score; a safety eval wants an upper bound on what a determined user could get, so weak elicitation is the failure that matters.

      The second problem is the test-taker. Models increasingly notice when they are being evaluated: Anthropic reported that Claude Sonnet 4.5 said so in about 13% of its automated audit transcripts. Eval awareness and deliberate sandbagging both make a model look safer under test than in use (lower capability in capability evals, better behaviour in propensity evals), which is why propensity evals now use realistic environments and why labs probe the model's internal representation of "this is a test".

      A simulation over 30 hard tasks with mixed per-task difficulty. The red line is a threshold set for this simulation: half the tasks solved. The orange bar is what a determined user would get with the best scaffold, 100 attempts and no sandbagging. The blue bar is what your eval measured with the settings above. Start with a bare prompt and one attempt, then add elicitation until the blue bar meets the orange; then let the model notice the test.

      11 · HISTORY AND FRONTIER

      Seventy-five years of trying to grade a machine

      The pattern repeats: a benchmark is built to be hard, becomes the target, saturates or leaks, and is replaced by one that is harder, fresher, more private or closer to real work. What changed after 2024 is that evaluation became its own field, with statistics, contamination audits and agent harnesses, rather than a table at the end of a paper.

      Where 2026 stands

      Static knowledge tests are retired as frontier signals; MMLU and GSM8K are sanity checks. SWE-bench Verified went in February 2026, when OpenAI stopped reporting it, citing flawed tests and contamination, and pointed to SWE-bench Pro. The live frontier is threefold: long-horizon agent evals (METR's suite is close to its ceiling and being extended), expert-graded real work (GDPval-style tasks across occupations), and rubric evals graded by calibrated judges. Underneath, two problems are not solved: keeping test sets out of training data as more of the web is model-written, and measuring models that increasingly know when they are being measured.

      12 · WHY IT MATTERS TO ME

      Your own evals are the only ones about your problem

      Public benchmarks pick which models are worth trying. They cannot tell you which one handles your tickets, your codebase or your review comments. That takes a golden set of your real cases, a grader you have checked against your own labels, and a paired comparison every time a prompt, model or tool changes. Pick the situation.
