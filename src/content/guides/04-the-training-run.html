An interactive field guide

How a model is made: from raw web text to an assistant that reasons

A language model starts as a few hundred billion random numbers. Training is the process that turns them into something that can write, follow instructions and think through a problem. It happens in stages, each with its own data, its own goal and its own failure modes, and almost every behaviour you notice in a deployed model traces back to one of them. This page builds the whole run, from one gradient step to a reasoning model, with a real model training live below.

00 · PLAIN WORDS

Every term, in one breath

Read this first or come back to it. Every dotted word on the page links here.

Parameter, weight N
One of the numbers inside the model. A 70B model has seventy billion of them, arranged as matrices that multiply the token vectors from the attention guide. Training changes nothing else.The knobs on a mixing desk, except there are billions of them and nobody labels any.
Loss
One number that says how wrong the model's predictions were on a batch of text. For language models it is the average negative log-probability the model gave to the token that actually came next. Random guessing over 128k tokens scores 11.8; a good model scores around 2.The score in a game you want to lose.
Gradient
For every parameter, the direction and rate at which the loss would change if you nudged that one parameter. Computed exactly, for all N parameters at once, by backpropagation.Standing on a hillside blindfolded, feeling which way the ground slopes under each foot.
Gradient descent, training step
Move every parameter a small distance against its gradient, so the loss on this batch goes down a little. One step: read a batch, run the model forward, compute the loss, run backward for gradients, update. Repeat a million times.Walking downhill one small step at a time, re-checking the slope after each.
Learning rate η
How far to move per step. Too small and training crawls; too large and it overshoots and blows up. Typical peak values are around 0.0003 for a large model, and the rate is changed on a schedule during the run.Stride length. Long strides on open ground, baby steps near the bottom.
Batch
The chunk of text one step learns from, measured in tokens. Frontier runs use batches of 4M to 16M tokens, so one step is thousands of documents averaged together.Grading a stack of papers before adjusting how you teach, not after each one.
Epoch
One full pass over the training data. Pretraining usually makes about one epoch; everything is seen once. Fine-tuning on a small dataset makes several.Reading the textbook cover to cover, once.
Optimizer, Adam, AdamW
The rule that turns gradients into parameter updates. Plain gradient descent uses the gradient as is. Adam keeps a running average of each parameter's gradient and of its square, and scales the step so every parameter moves at a sensible speed. AdamW adds a small pull toward zero. Nearly every model since 2015 uses it.Cruise control for each knob: smooths the jitter, speeds up the sluggish ones.
Forward pass, backward pass
Forward: tokens in, predictions out, loss computed. Backward: the loss flows back through every layer, and the chain rule produces the gradient for every parameter. The backward pass costs about twice the forward, which is where the 6 in 6·N·D comes from (2 forward, 4 backward).Forward is taking the exam. Backward is the teacher marking it and telling every knob what it did wrong.
Pretraining
The first and by far the biggest stage: predict the next token over trillions of tokens of text. Produces a base model that knows language, facts and code but only continues text; it does not answer you.Reading the whole library before anyone tells you what the job is.
Corpus, Common Crawl
The training text. Most of it comes from Common Crawl, a non-profit's monthly snapshots of the public web, plus code from GitHub, books, papers, Wikipedia and licensed or purchased sets. Raw crawl is mostly junk; most of the work is filtering it.A landfill with a library buried in it.
Deduplication, quality filtering
Removing repeated pages (exact, near-duplicate and URL-level) and scoring the rest for quality with heuristics and small classifiers. FineWeb turned 96 crawl snapshots into 15T tokens this way. Dedup and filtering improve a model more than almost any architecture change.Sorting the landfill. Most of the model's quality is decided here, before any GPU turns on.
Data mixture
The proportions of web, code, math, books, multilingual and synthetic text in the batch. Set by experiments on small models; changed near the end of the run to lean on the highest-quality sources.A diet. Code is the vegetables: everyone adds more than tastes natural because it makes the model think better.
Scaling law
An empirical rule that loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters and tokens, so you can predict a big model's loss from small ones. Kaplan et al. found it in 2020; Chinchilla corrected the recipe in 2022.A weather forecast for a run you cannot afford to do twice.
Compute-optimal, Chinchilla
For a fixed compute budget, the best loss comes from training about 20 tokens per parameter. Models meant to be served cheaply are deliberately "over-trained" far past that (Llama 3 8B: 1,875 tokens per parameter) because a smaller model is cheaper every time it answers.Spend the budget on a longer education for a smaller brain, if the brain has to work for a living.
FLOPs, 6ND
Floating-point operations. A training run costs about 6 × parameters × tokens: 6 × 405B × 15T ≈ 4 × 10²⁵ for Llama 3 405B. Divide by what the GPUs actually deliver and you get the calendar.The electricity bill, counted in multiplications.
Mixed precision, bf16, FP8
Doing the matrix math in 16-bit (or 8-bit) numbers for speed while keeping a 32-bit master copy of the parameters for the small updates. DeepSeek-V3 was the first large run in FP8.Sketching in pencil, keeping the ledger in ink.
Data, tensor, pipeline parallelism
Three ways to split a run over thousands of GPUs: copy the model and split the batch (data), split one matrix across GPUs (tensor), or give each GPU a slice of layers (pipeline). Real runs use all three at once.Many kitchens (data), many cooks per dish (tensor), an assembly line of stations (pipeline).
ZeRO, FSDP, sharding
Splitting the optimizer state, gradients and parameters across the data-parallel GPUs instead of copying them, so each holds 1/n of the 16 bytes per parameter. Makes a 400B-parameter run fit at all.One shared ledger with each accountant holding a few pages, instead of everyone holding the whole book.
Checkpoint
A saved copy of all parameters and optimizer state, written every few hours. Hardware fails constantly at scale (Llama 3: 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days); training resumes from the last checkpoint.Saving the game.
Loss spike, divergence
The loss suddenly jumping, sometimes never recovering. Caused by a bad batch, a too-large learning rate or numerical overflow. Fixed by gradient clipping, rewinding to a checkpoint and skipping the batch.Tripping on the stairs. Usually you catch yourself; sometimes you fall the whole flight.
Learning-rate schedule, warmup, cosine, WSD
The learning rate starts near zero and ramps up (warmup), holds or follows a cosine curve, then decays. Warmup-stable-decay (WSD) holds it flat and only decays at the very end, which lets you keep training without deciding the end date in advance.Easing into a run, cruising, then slowing to a walk before you stop.
Annealing, mid-training
The last stretch of pretraining, where the learning rate decays and the mixture shifts to the best data: textbooks, math, code, long documents, some synthetic text. A lot of a model's benchmark score is bought here.Cramming from the good notes the week before the exam.
Base model
The model straight out of pretraining. Give it "What is the capital of France?" and it may continue with "What is the capital of Germany?" because that is what quiz pages look like. All the knowledge, none of the manners.A brilliant autocomplete that has never been told it is talking to someone.
Supervised fine-tuning SFT
Continue training the base model on example conversations written by people (and increasingly by other models), computing loss only on the assistant's turns. Teaches the format of being an assistant in a few thousand to a million examples.Showing a new hire a hundred good emails before letting them write one.
Chat template, special tokens
The fixed wrapping that marks who is speaking: tokens like <|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>. Introduced at SFT. The API hides them from you; the model never sees a conversation without them.Stage directions in a script. The actors never say them aloud, but they need them to know whose line it is.
Reinforcement learning from human feedback RLHF
Show people pairs of answers, record which they prefer, train a reward model to predict that preference, then train the chat model to produce answers the reward model scores highly while staying close to where it started. InstructGPT, 2022; the step that made ChatGPT feel like ChatGPT.Learning a reader's taste from thumbs up and down instead of from a rulebook.
Reward model, preference pair
A copy of the model with its last layer replaced by a single score. Trained so that the preferred answer in each pair scores higher than the rejected one (the Bradley-Terry model: P(A beats B) = σ(rA − rB)). It is a stand-in for the human, and it can be fooled.A critic trained on your thumbs. It knows what you liked; it does not know why.
KL penalty, reference model
A term in the RL objective that charges the model for drifting from a frozen copy of itself (the reference). Without it the model finds answers that score high with the reward model and read like nonsense to a human.A leash. Long enough to explore, short enough not to run into traffic.
PPO, policy, rollout
Proximal policy optimization, the RL algorithm InstructGPT used. The "policy" is just the model being trained; a "rollout" is one sampled answer. PPO limits how much each update changes the policy and needs a fourth model (the critic) to estimate how good a partial answer is.Four models in memory, one being taught. Expensive, fiddly, effective.
Direct preference optimization DPO
A 2023 shortcut: skip the reward model and the RL loop; train directly on the preference pairs with a loss that pushes the preferred answer's probability up and the rejected one's down, relative to the reference. One model, one pass, most of the benefit.Learning taste straight from the thumbs, without first training a critic to imitate them.
RL with verifiable rewards RLVR
Reinforcement learning where the reward is a program, not a model: does the final answer match? Do the tests pass? Did the tool call succeed? No reward model to fool. The recipe behind every reasoning model since late 2024.Practising on problems with an answer key instead of asking a critic how it felt.
GRPO, advantage
Group relative policy optimization (DeepSeek, 2024). Sample a group of answers to the same prompt, score each, subtract the group's mean: answers above average get pushed up, below average pushed down. No critic model needed. The "advantage" is that signed, normalized score.Grading on a curve, then telling each attempt whether it beat the class.
Reasoning model, chain of thought
A model trained with RLVR to write out intermediate steps before the answer. The steps are ordinary tokens; what changed is that RL rewarded whatever thinking made the final answer right, so the model learned to check, backtrack and try again. More thinking tokens, better answers: "test-time compute".Showing your working, because the ones who showed their working got the marks.
Reward hacking, Goodhart
The model finding ways to raise the reward that are not what the designer meant: longer answers, confident tone, flattery, special-casing the tests. Any proxy pushed hard enough stops measuring what it was built to measure.Teaching to the test until the test stops meaning anything.
LoRA, QLoRA
Low-rank adaptation: freeze the model and train two thin matrices per layer whose product is added to the original weight. Trains under 1% of the parameters, fits on one GPU, and the result is a small file you can swap in. QLoRA does the same over a 4-bit quantized base.A clip-on lens instead of regrinding the camera's glass.
Distillation
Training a smaller student model on the outputs (or full probability distributions) of a larger teacher. Most small models you can run locally are distilled from something bigger; the reasoning in DeepSeek-R1's 7B variants came this way.Learning from the professor's lecture notes instead of rediscovering the field.
Synthetic data, model collapse
Training text written by another model: rewritten web pages, generated math problems, dialogues. Now a large share of post-training data and a growing share of pretraining. The risk is a model trained mostly on model output losing the tails of real human text over generations.Photocopies of photocopies. Fine once, fuzzy by the fifth.
Overfitting, held-out set
Training loss keeps falling while loss on text the model never saw starts rising: it is memorising. The held-out (validation) set is kept apart from training precisely to catch this. Benchmarks leaking into training data (contamination) is the same disease.Memorising last year's exam. Great on the practice paper, useless in the hall.
Catastrophic forgetting
Fine-tuning on your narrow data erodes what the model knew before. Mitigated by small learning rates, LoRA, mixing in general data and not training for long.Learning a new phone number by overwriting your old one.
Mixture of experts MoE
Each layer's feed-forward block is replaced by many (say 256) smaller blocks and a router that sends each token to a few of them. The model has far more parameters than it uses per token: DeepSeek-V3 has 671B, uses 37B per token. Cheaper to train and serve per unit of quality; harder to train stably.A hospital with specialists and a triage desk, instead of one doctor who knows everything.
Benchmark, eval
A fixed set of questions with known answers scored during and after training: MMLU for knowledge, GSM8K and MATH for arithmetic, HumanEval and SWE-bench for code. Guides every decision in the run, and is exactly what reward hacking and contamination corrupt. The next guide is about these.The exam. Load-bearing and gameable at the same time.

01 · WHAT LEARNING IS

One number, pushed downhill

Strip away the scale and training is a single idea. The model has parameters, billions of numbers that start random. You give it a batch of text with the last token hidden, it predicts a probability for every token in the vocabulary (the logits from the token guide), and you compute the loss: how little probability it put on the token that was actually there. Then comes the trick that makes all of this possible. Backpropagation computes, for every single parameter at once, how the loss would change if that parameter moved a hair. That is the gradient. Move every parameter a small step the other way, and the loss on that batch drops a little.

That is one training step. A frontier run makes about a million of them. The learning rate decides how big each step is, and the instrument below shows why it is the most important dial in the room: too small and you crawl; too large and you leap over the valley and land higher than you started.

steps 0position 2.60loss —slope —step size —

A loss landscape with one parameter instead of billions. The ball only ever knows the slope under it (the gradient) and moves against it by learning rate × slope. Start on the right and it settles in the shallow valley unless the rate is big enough to carry it over the bump. Push the rate past about 0.3 and watch it bounce out entirely: that is a loss spike. Real landscapes have billions of axes, but the step rule is this one, applied to each.

Two refinements make this work at scale. First, no one uses the raw gradient. Adam keeps a running average of each parameter's recent gradients and of their squares, and divides one by the other, so a parameter whose gradient is tiny but consistent moves as fast as one whose gradient is huge and noisy. Switch the hero instrument to Adam and it reaches the same loss in a fraction of the steps. Second, you never compute the loss on one example. A batch of millions of tokens is averaged, so each step is a stable estimate of the true downhill direction rather than a lurch toward whatever one document wanted.

02 · THE DATA

Fifteen trillion tokens, and where they come from

The loss is only as good as the text it is computed on, and this is where most of a lab's effort now goes. The raw material is the public web, mostly via Common Crawl's monthly snapshots, plus code repositories, books, academic papers, Wikipedia and whatever can be licensed. Raw crawl is overwhelmingly junk: boilerplate, navigation menus, spam, machine-translated pages, the same press release on four hundred sites. The pipeline that turns it into training text is a funnel of filters, and each one is a small research project.

Text is extracted from HTML. A language classifier keeps the languages you want. Deduplication removes exact repeats, near-repeats (by hashing shingles of words) and whole repeated URLs; without it the model memorises the popular pages and learns less from everything else. Quality filters drop pages with too little punctuation, too many lists, too much adult content, and increasingly a small model scores each page for "educational value" or similarity to good text. What survives, FineWeb's 15T tokens from 96 snapshots for example, is the pretraining set. Then comes the mixture: how much code, how much math, how much of each language. Set by experiments on small models, it is one of a lab's most guarded numbers.

When a source has to be read more than about four times, the bar turns orange: Muennighoff et al. 2023 found that repeats beyond that add little. The "data wall" is this picture at 50T tokens.

Two facts about the data decide a lot of what you see later. The model learns about one epoch of this: each token once. So anything rare on the web is rare in its head, and anything common is over-learned. And the mixture changes near the end of the run, when the highest-quality sources are leaned on; that annealing phase is where a surprising share of benchmark performance is bought, and where the first synthetic text usually enters.

03 · HOW BIG, HOW LONG

Scaling laws: forecasting a run you can only afford once

Before a lab spends tens of millions of dollars on a run, it needs to know what it will get. The answer is a scaling law: train a ladder of small models, fit a curve, extrapolate. Loss falls as a power law in both parameters and tokens, smoothly enough that a 2020 paper could predict GPT-3's loss before it was trained. The compute a run needs is close to 6 × N × D, six operations per parameter per token, and so the question becomes: for a fixed budget, do you want a bigger model that reads less, or a smaller one that reads more?

Kaplan et al. in 2020 said bigger model. Chinchilla in 2022 reran the experiment with properly tuned learning rates and said the balance is about 20 tokens per parameter: GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) had been badly undertrained, and a 70B model on 1.4T tokens beat it on the same budget. Then the industry walked past that number on purpose. A model is trained once and served billions of times, and a smaller model is cheaper every time it answers, so Llama 3 8B was trained on 15T tokens, nearly a hundred times the Chinchilla ratio. Slightly worse loss than the compute-optimal choice, far cheaper to run.

04 · THE MACHINERY

Fitting 400 billion parameters on hardware that holds 80 gigabytes

Here is the first hard physical fact. Training needs far more memory per parameter than inference. In mixed precision, each parameter is stored twice (a 16-bit working copy and a 32-bit master copy that accumulates the tiny updates), its gradient needs 2 more bytes, and Adam's two running averages need 8 more. Sixteen bytes per parameter, before a single activation. A 405B model therefore needs about 6.5 TB just for its training state, against 80 GB on an H100. No single device comes close, and so every large run is a distributed-systems problem first and a machine-learning problem second.

Three kinds of parallelism split the work. Data parallelism copies the model to many groups of GPUs and gives each a different slice of the batch; after the backward pass they average their gradients. Tensor parallelism splits one layer's giant matrices across a few GPUs in the same server, which have very fast links between them. Pipeline parallelism gives each group of GPUs a slice of the layers and passes activations down the line like an assembly line. ZeRO and FSDP make the data-parallel copies share one set of optimizer state and gradients instead of duplicating it, which is what gets the 16 bytes per parameter divided by the number of GPUs.

Training-state memory only; activations add more and are usually handled by recomputing them in the backward pass (trading compute for memory). "Minimum GPUs" is the count needed to hold the state once, fully sharded, leaving a quarter of each device free for activations and workspace. Llama 3 405B was trained with tensor 8 × pipeline 16 × data 128 = 16,384 GPUs.

Then the machines break. Llama 3's team logged 419 unexpected interruptions over 54 days, more than half from GPU faults. Runs survive on checkpoints, saved every few hours, and on tooling that detects a dead node, swaps it and resumes within minutes. Add numerical trouble: 16-bit arithmetic overflows, a bad batch produces an enormous gradient, and the loss spikes. Gradient clipping (capping the update size), skipping the offending batch and rewinding to a checkpoint are routine. A large run is babysat around the clock by people watching a loss curve.

05 · THE DIALS

The learning-rate schedule, and why the last 10% of a run matters so much

Chapter 01 said the learning rate is the most important dial. In practice it is not a number but a schedule. A fresh model's gradients are wild, so the rate starts near zero and ramps up over the first few thousand steps (warmup); a large rate at step one is the quickest way to a dead run. It then runs at its peak, which for a big model is around 0.0003, and decays toward zero by the end. The decay is not cosmetic. While the rate is high the parameters jitter around the valley floor; lowering it lets them settle, and the loss drops sharply in the final stretch. That drop is where the annealing phase from chapter 02 lives: the labs lower the rate and switch to their best data at the same moment.

The classic shape is warmup then a cosine curve to 10% of peak. Its weakness is that you must choose the total length in advance, because the cosine is pinned to it. Warmup-stable-decay (WSD) keeps the rate flat for as long as you like and only decays over the final 10–20%, so a lab can train longer than planned, or branch several decayed models off one long stable run. It also recovers almost the whole cosine benefit, which is why it spread quickly after 2024.

Blue is the learning rate over the run; orange is a stylised loss curve that responds to it: the loss drops fastest in the decay phase, and a constant rate leaves gains on the table. The loss shape is a sketch of the behaviour reported by Hägele et al. 2024 and the MiniCPM team, not a fit. Try a tiny warmup with the highest peak and notice the spike early on.

The other dials are fewer than you would think. Batch size, which grows during a run as the gradients get less noisy. Weight decay, the gentle pull toward zero in AdamW. Gradient clipping at 1.0, nearly universal. And the dozens of architecture choices (depth, width, number of attention heads, MoE expert count) that are fixed by small-model sweeps before the big run starts and never changed. Hyperparameter tuning at full scale does not exist; you get one run.

06 · BASE TO ASSISTANT

Supervised fine-tuning: teaching it that someone is talking to it

After pretraining you have a base model, and it is strange. Ask it a question and it may continue with more questions, because on the web a question is usually followed by another question. Ask it to summarise and it might write "Summary:" and then a new article. It has read everything and has no idea it is in a conversation. The first post-training stage fixes exactly this, and it is almost embarrassingly small: a few thousand to a million example conversations, a few hours on a few dozen GPUs, with the same next-token loss as pretraining.

Two details carry the whole trick. First, the chat template: special tokens that mark where the system prompt, the user's turn and the assistant's turn begin and end. The model learns that after the assistant header it should answer, and after the end-of-turn token it should stop. Second, the loss mask: the loss is computed only on the assistant's tokens. The model is not asked to predict what the user will say, only how to reply. That is all SFT is. It teaches the format and the manner. It cannot add knowledge the base model lacks, and if the examples are mediocre, the assistant will be mediocre in exactly the same way.

Where do the examples come from? Early on, contractors wrote them: InstructGPT's 13,000 demonstrations. Llama 2 found that tens of thousands of carefully written examples beat millions of scraped ones. Now most SFT data is synthetic: a stronger model writes answers, often with rejection sampling (generate several, keep the one a checker or judge model likes), and humans edit a fraction. A side effect is that small open models inherit the voice of whichever frontier model wrote their training set.

07 · LEARNING TASTE

Preference tuning: the difference between a correct answer and a good one

SFT teaches the model to imitate. But the best answer is often not something a person can write down in advance; it is easier to recognise than to produce. "Which of these two is better?" is a question anyone can answer in seconds, and RLHF is the machinery for turning millions of those seconds into a model. Show a person two answers to the same prompt. Record the winner. Train a reward model, a copy of the model whose last layer outputs one number, so that winners score higher than losers. Then train the chat model to produce answers that score well.

The danger is immediate. If you optimise against a learned score hard enough, you find its blind spots: the reward model likes long answers, so the model gets verbose; it likes confidence, so the model stops hedging even when it should; it likes agreement, so the model flatters you. The fix is the KL penalty: a charge for drifting from the SFT model you started with. The optimal policy under that objective has a clean form (the reference probabilities multiplied by ereward/β), and the instrument below is that formula. InstructGPT did this with PPO, which needs four models in memory and weeks of tuning. In 2023 DPO showed you can skip the reward model and the RL loop and train on the pairs directly, with most of the benefit. Most open models use DPO or a cousin; the frontier labs still mostly run reward models and RL.

08 · LEARNING TO REASON

Reinforcement learning with an answer key

Everything in chapter 07 depended on a judge who could be fooled. In 2024 the field noticed that for a large class of problems you do not need one. A math problem has an answer; code has tests; a tool call either worked or did not. RL with verifiable rewards replaces the reward model with a program: sample an answer, check it, reward 1 or 0. There is nothing to flatter. And when you run this loop over hundreds of thousands of problems, something happens that SFT never produced: the model starts writing longer and longer solutions, checking its own steps, saying "wait, that's wrong" and backtracking, because the attempts that did those things were the ones that got the reward. That is what a reasoning model is. The thinking is ordinary tokens; what is new is the training signal that selected for it.

The algorithm most of these runs use is GRPO. For each prompt, sample a group of answers, say eight. Score each. Subtract the group's average and divide by its spread, so that answers better than their siblings get a positive advantage and worse ones get a negative one. Push up the probability of every token in the positive answers, push down the negative ones. No critic model, no reward model: just the policy, a frozen reference and a checker. DeepSeek-R1 in January 2025 showed the whole thing working in the open, including a model (R1-Zero) trained with RL alone from a base model that learned to reason without a single hand-written example.

Prompt: a train leaves at 9:40 and arrives at 13:15. How long is the trip?

Eight rollouts of the same prompt; click any row to flip whether the checker accepted it. Rewards are 1 or 0, the baseline is the group mean, and the bar is each rollout's advantage. When all eight pass or all eight fail the advantages are zero and the prompt teaches nothing, which is why RLVR pipelines filter for problems the model solves some of the time. Tick the length bonus and watch the advantage of a long wrong answer turn positive: that is how reasoning models learned to ramble before labs fixed their rewards.

Two consequences shape what you see when you use these models. Rewarding only the final answer means the model is free to think however works, and the chains of thought are sometimes odd, switch languages or loop; labs add small format rewards and a readability pass to tame them. And the amount of thinking is now a dial. More sampled tokens at inference buys accuracy, which is why reasoning models have a "thinking budget", and why the speed guide's output-token costs matter more than ever. Capability was once bought entirely at training time; since late 2024 part of it is bought per request.

09 · TUNING IT YOURSELF

Fine-tuning on your data: when, how, and what it costs

Everything so far was done by a lab. The stage you can do yourself is fine-tuning: continue training an open model (or a hosted one through an API) on your own examples. The first question is whether you should. Fine-tuning changes how the model behaves, not what it knows; it is the right tool for a fixed format, a house style, a narrow classification task or a smaller model that must imitate a bigger one. It is the wrong tool for teaching facts that change, which is what retrieval and the context window are for, and it is rarely needed when a good prompt gets you 95% of the way. The usual order is prompt, then retrieval, then fine-tune, then only if the first two have been measured and found wanting.

If you do, you will almost certainly use LoRA. Full fine-tuning of a 70B model needs the 16 bytes per parameter from chapter 04, over a terabyte of GPU memory. LoRA freezes the model and trains two thin matrices per weight, a tall one and a wide one, whose product is added to the original. With a rank of 16 that is well under 1% of the parameters, the optimizer state shrinks to match, and the result is a file of a few hundred megabytes that can be swapped onto the base model at serving time (an inference engine can even serve many adapters at once). QLoRA does the same over a 4-bit quantized base, which puts a 70B fine-tune on a single 48 GB card. Quality is close to full fine-tuning for most tasks and the risk of catastrophic forgetting is lower, because the base is untouched.

Two other tools belong here. Distillation: generate answers (or full chains of thought) with a large model and fine-tune a small one on them; it is how a 7B model gets reasoning it could never have learned from scratch, and it is how most of the small models you can run locally were made. And preference tuning on your own data with DPO, when "better" is easier to click than to write. In all cases the rules from chapter 10 apply with extra force: hold out a test set before you start, and measure the base model on it first, because the most common result of a first fine-tune is a model slightly worse than the prompt you had.

10 · MEASURING

Held-out loss, benchmarks and the thing you must never train on

Every decision in a run is made by looking at a number, and the whole art is picking numbers that cannot lie to you. The first is held-out loss: the same next-token loss, computed on text set aside before training and never trained on. While training loss and held-out loss fall together, the model is learning; when training loss keeps falling and held-out loss turns up, the model has started memorising its training set. In pretraining this rarely happens because each token is seen once. In fine-tuning on a few thousand examples it happens by the third epoch, and it is the first thing to check when a fine-tuned model disappoints.

The second number is benchmarks: fixed question sets with known answers, scored at every checkpoint. MMLU for knowledge, GSM8K and MATH for arithmetic, HumanEval and SWE-bench for code, and dozens more. They tell you whether the loss improvements mean anything, and whether a stage made the model better at the things people want. They also leak. Benchmark questions are on the web, so they are in Common Crawl, so a model can score well by having read the test (contamination); labs decontaminate by searching their corpus for the test strings, imperfectly. And when a benchmark becomes a target it stops being a measure, as chapter 11 shows. The next guide in this series is about evaluation; here it is enough to know that training is steered entirely by these two kinds of number.

A sketch of fine-tuning dynamics, not a measurement: training loss (blue) always falls; held-out loss (orange) bottoms out and then climbs as the model memorises. Fewer examples, a bigger model and a higher learning rate all bring the turn earlier. The dashed line marks where to stop. Keep the test set out of the training set and out of the prompts you tune by hand; the moment you look at it to make a decision, it has started to leak.

11 · TRAPS AND THE FRONTIER

Nine years of the same loop, and the places it leaks

Every stage on this page optimises a number, and every number can be gamed. The sharpest version is the reward hacking curve: the model's score keeps rising while the thing you actually wanted peaks and declines.

Where 2026 stands

Pretraining is still the bulk of the compute, but it has stopped being the whole story. The best public data has been read; growth now comes from synthetic and curated data, from mixture-of-experts architectures that buy parameters cheaply, from FP8 and new optimizers that buy steps cheaply, and above all from reinforcement learning, whose share of a frontier training budget has gone from a rounding error to a meaningful fraction in two years. The open questions are the ones this page kept bumping into: how far RL on verifiable tasks generalises to tasks nobody can verify; whether models trained on model output keep the richness of the human text underneath; and whether continual learning, a model that keeps training after deployment, can be made safe enough to ship. Chapter 12 is what to do with all this on a Tuesday.

12 · WHY IT MATTERS TO ME

Reading a model's behaviour back to the stage that caused it

The practical payoff of knowing the pipeline is diagnosis. When a model does something odd, the odd thing almost always has an address. Click a behaviour.

Some things to carry into your own work. When the model does not know something recent, that is pretraining's cutoff and no prompt fixes it; retrieve. When it is verbose or agreeable, that is preference tuning, and a system prompt that asks for terse, disagreeing answers is working against a trained prior, so expect to repeat yourself. When a reasoning model takes long on an easy question, that is RL having learned that thinking pays; give it a budget. When you fine-tune, hold out a test set first, measure the base, use LoRA, stop at the held-out minimum, and expect your first attempt to lose to a good prompt. When a benchmark number looks too good, ask what the training set contained. And when a model's manners differ from a competitor's, you are mostly looking at the last 5% of its compute.

Finally, the connection to the earlier guides. The attention guide is what the forward pass does; the speed guide is what the forward pass costs; the token guide is what goes in and comes out. This page is how the numbers inside got their values.
