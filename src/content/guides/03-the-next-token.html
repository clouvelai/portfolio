An interactive field guide

Tokens, and how the model picks the next one

A language model does not read letters or write words. It reads a list of numbered pieces called tokens, and at every step it produces one score for every piece it knows, then something rolls a die weighted by those scores. Everything you pay for, every speed limit, every "temperature" setting and every JSON-that-came-back-broken lives in those two facts. This page builds both from scratch.

00 · PLAIN WORDS

Every term, in one breath

The chapters use these words freely. Each entry is a one-sentence definition and a picture to hold it by. Dotted-underlined terms in the chapters link back here. Nothing on this page assumes you know any of them already.

Token
The unit a model reads and writes: a whole word, a piece of a word, a run of digits, a punctuation mark, sometimes a single byte. "Tokenization" is the deterministic rule that chops text into these.Lego bricks of text. The model only ever sees bricks, never the plastic they are made of.
Vocabulary V
The fixed list of every token a model knows, each with an integer id. GPT-2 had 50,257; GPT-4 about 100k; GPT-4o and Llama 3 about 200k and 128k; Gemma 3 about 262k.The brick catalogue. Bigger catalogue, fewer bricks per sentence, bigger box to search.
Byte-pair encoding BPE
The recipe most vocabularies are built with: start from single bytes, repeatedly find the most frequent neighbouring pair in a training corpus, glue it into a new token, repeat tens of thousands of times.Watching which bricks always get snapped together and selling them pre-glued.
Merge, merge rank
One glueing step in BPE. Merges are numbered in the order they were learned, and that order is replayed exactly when new text is tokenized, so tokenization is deterministic.The recipe card, numbered. Follow it in order and everyone gets the same bricks.
Byte, UTF-8, byte fallback
Text in a computer is bytes; UTF-8 writes an English letter in one byte and most other scripts in two to four. Byte-level BPE keeps all 256 single bytes as tokens, so any string can be tokenized, just inefficiently.A floor of 256 tiny bricks that can build anything, slowly.
Pre-tokenization
A regular expression that splits text into chunks (words with their leading space, runs of digits, punctuation) before BPE runs inside each chunk. It is why tokens rarely cross a space and why numbers come out in 1–3 digit pieces.Cutting the dough into rough lumps before the real cutter runs.
Special tokens, chat template
Reserved ids that never come from text: beginning-of-sequence, end-of-turn, role markers. The chat template is the fixed way a conversation is laid out with them before the model sees it.Stage directions printed in a different ink than the dialogue.
Fertility tokens per word
How many tokens a word costs on average. About 1.3 for English prose; 2–3 for many European and Asian languages; 5–10 for scripts the vocabulary was barely trained on. Also called the token premium.Exchange rate between words and bricks. It is not the same in every country.
Embedding
The table that turns each token id into a vector of a few thousand numbers; the first thing the model does with a token. Learned during training; row i is the model's idea of token i.Looking a word up in a dictionary whose definitions are coordinates.
LM head unembedding
The last matrix: it takes the model's final vector for the current position and produces one score per vocabulary entry. In many models it is the embedding table reused backwards.The same dictionary, read in reverse: from coordinates back to every word, with a distance to each.
Logit
One raw score the LM head gives a token. Unbounded, meaningless alone; only differences between logits matter. There are V of them per step.Points on a scoreboard before anyone has turned them into odds.
Softmax
The function that turns logits into probabilities: raise e to each logit, divide by the total. Big logits get most of the mass; the gaps between logits set the ratios between probabilities.Turning the scoreboard into betting odds that add to 100%.
Probability distribution
The full list of V probabilities for the next token, summing to 1. It is the model's actual output. "The model said Paris" means the die landed on Paris.The weighted die itself, before it is thrown.
Temperature T
A number every logit is divided by before softmax. Below 1 sharpens the distribution toward the favourite; above 1 flattens it toward uniform; 0 means always take the top one.Loading the die. Low temperature glues it to one face.
Greedy decoding argmax
Always pick the highest-probability token. Equivalent to temperature 0. Repetitive on open-ended text, ideal for code, extraction and tool calls.Never rolling, just reading the heaviest face.
Sampling
Drawing a token at random in proportion to its probability. With a random number generator seeded the same way, the same draw repeats; otherwise each call is a new roll.Actually throwing the die.
Top-k
Before sampling, keep only the k most likely tokens and renormalize. Simple, but k is blind to shape: 40 is too many when the model is sure, too few when it is torn.Only the first k horses are allowed to run, whatever the odds.
Top-p nucleus
Keep the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to p (often 0.9–0.95), then renormalize. Adapts: a confident step keeps one token, a torn step keeps dozens.Let horses run until the odds in the field add up to 90%.
Min-p
Keep every token whose probability is at least a fraction (say 0.1) of the top token's. Scales with confidence like top-p, and behaves well at high temperature where top-p lets junk in.Anyone within a tenth of the favourite's odds may run.
Seed
The starting value of the random number generator used for sampling. Same seed, same model, same hardware path: same rolls. It controls the dice, not the scores.Shuffling a deck the same way twice.
Nondeterminism
Why identical requests at temperature 0 can still differ: floating-point sums come out slightly differently depending on how the server batched the work, and a near-tie flips. Fixable with batch-invariant kernels, at some speed cost.Two accountants adding the same column in different orders and disagreeing in the last cent.
Repetition, frequency, presence penalties
Adjustments that lower the logits of tokens already in the text. Frequency scales with how often; presence is a flat hit once a token has appeared; repetition penalty divides or multiplies the logit instead.Charging a fee each time the model reaches for the same brick.
Logit bias
A number you add to a specific token's logit on every step. Large negative forbids it; large positive forces it. The bluntest possible steering.A thumb on one face of the die.
Stop sequence, max tokens, EOS
Three ways a generation ends: the model emits its end-of-sequence token, a string you listed appears, or the output hits the length cap. A cap hit is a truncation, not an ending.The author stops, the editor stops them, or the paper runs out.
Constrained decoding structured output · grammar
Zeroing out every token the grammar (a JSON schema, a regex, a tool signature) would not allow at this position, before sampling. The output is then valid by construction, every time.A stencil over the die: faces outside the stencil cannot land.
Logprob
The natural log of a token's probability. 0 means certain; −0.69 is a coin flip; −4.6 is 1%. Returned per token by most APIs on request. Summing them gives the probability of the whole sequence.The odds, written on a scale where multiplying becomes adding.
Entropy
How spread out a distribution is, in nats or bits. Zero when one token holds all the mass; ln V when every token is equally likely. High entropy steps are where sampling decisions actually matter.How many faces the die effectively has.
Perplexity
e to the average negative logprob over a text: the effective number of equally likely choices the model felt it had per token. A model that scores 5 on a text was, on average, choosing among five.Average number of plausible next bricks. Lower means the text felt more predictable.
Beam search
Keep the b most probable partial sequences at every step instead of one. Finds higher-probability wholes; produces bland, repetitive text for open prompts. Rare in chat, common in translation.Exploring the b best paths through the maze at once.
Token healing, prompt boundary
A prompt that ends mid-token (a trailing space, half a URL) forces the model onto a path training rarely showed it. Token healing backs up one token and lets the model regenerate it with the boundary free.Unsnapping the last brick so the next one can attach where it naturally would.
Glitch tokens
Vocabulary entries that appeared in the tokenizer's training text but almost never in the model's, so their embeddings were never really trained. Feed one in and the model behaves strangely.A brick in the catalogue that no one ever built with.
Speculative decoding
A fast draft model proposes several tokens; the big model checks them in one pass and keeps the ones it would have sampled. Exactly the same distribution as sampling from the big model alone, by a rejection-sampling rule.A junior finishing your sentences; you nod through the right ones.
Multi-token prediction MTP
Training the model with extra heads that predict tokens 2, 3, 4 steps ahead. Used as a built-in draft for speculative decoding, and arguably as a better training signal.Writing the next three words in pencil, then checking them in ink.
Tokenizer-free models MegaByte · BLT · H-Net
Architectures that read raw bytes and learn their own grouping as they go, instead of using a fixed vocabulary. Research-stage in 2025–2026, aimed at every trap in chapter 11.Letting the builder decide the brick sizes per job.

01 · WHAT A TOKEN IS

The model never sees letters

Type a sentence into a chat box and the first thing that happens is not intelligence. A small, fixed program called the tokenizer chops your text into pieces from a list it was given years earlier, and replaces each piece with its number in that list. The model receives the numbers. It never sees the letters inside a piece, which is why a frontier system can write a sonnet and still miscount the r's in "strawberry".

The pieces are chosen by frequency, not by meaning. Common words are one token, often with their leading space glued on (" the" and "the" are different tokens). Rare words break into familiar fragments. Anything the vocabulary has never seen falls back to raw bytes, one token each, so nothing is ever untokenizable, just expensive. Try it: the box below holds a tiny real tokenizer, trained from scratch in your browser on the three kilobytes of text in this page's script, so it behaves like a big one at one-thousandth the scale.

Each box is one token; a dashed box is a raw byte fallback the toy vocabulary had no merge for. Spaces ride along as "␣" at the front of a word, exactly as in GPT-style tokenizers. Real vocabularies are 100k–260k entries trained on terabytes, so real splits are coarser than these, but the mechanism is identical. Type something in another language, or paste some code.

Two habits worth building now. First, think in tokens whenever you think about cost or length: a context window, a price, a speed are all per token. Second, when a model fails at something that looks trivially easy, ask whether the failure is in the model or in the pieces it was handed.

02 · BUILDING A VOCABULARY

Glue the most common pair, repeat fifty thousand times

Nobody writes the vocabulary by hand. Byte-pair encoding starts with the 256 possible bytes as its only tokens, runs over a huge pile of text, and asks one question: which two neighbouring tokens occur together most often? It glues that pair into a new token, replaces every occurrence, and asks again. Each answer is a merge, and the merges are numbered.

Stop after 50,000 merges and you have GPT-2's vocabulary; after 100,000, something like GPT-4's. The numbered merge list is the whole tokenizer. New text is tokenized by replaying the merges in order inside each chunk, so the same text always yields the same tokens. Drag the slider to replay the training that happened when this page loaded.

The sample sentence is re-tokenized with only the first k merges. Watch the byte count stay fixed while the token count falls: compression is the whole point. The merges learned first are the ones you would guess (" the", "ing", "tion"); the last ones are rare and specific. Real tokenizers add one trick before all this, a regular expression that pre-splits text so merges never cross a space and digits group in threes.

Three consequences. The vocabulary mirrors its training text: English and code are compressed beautifully, Burmese barely at all. The merge order is frozen forever, so a model's tokenizer is older than the model and cannot learn new words after launch. And vocabulary size is a trade: bigger vocabularies give fewer tokens per sentence and a faster, cheaper model per word, but a larger output layer and rarer, worse-trained entries at the tail.

03 · THE BILL

You are charged per piece, and the pieces are not the same size everywhere

Prices, context windows, rate limits and speeds are all quoted per token, so the real unit cost of your text is tokens per word, the fertility. For English prose it is about 1.3. For most other languages it is higher, because the vocabulary spent most of its merges on English and code. The same paragraph can cost twice as much in German, three times in Hindi, and ten times in a script the tokenizer barely saw, in money, in context space and in time.

Two other places the bill surprises people. Numbers: most tokenizers chunk digits in groups of up to three, so 1234567 is three tokens and 1,234,567 is five, and arithmetic across chunk boundaries is a known weak spot. And structure: JSON, XML and Markdown tables spend tokens on quotes, braces, indentation and repeated keys, so a structured answer costs about half again what its facts would cost as prose. Estimate your own bill below.

Multipliers are approximate, for 100k-class vocabularies. The bar for English is the same text at fertility 1.3; the gap is the token premium, paid on every request and again in the context window. For a long-running agent the premium compounds, because the same system prompt and history are re-sent every turn.

04 · NUMBERS IN, NUMBERS OUT

A row in, a score for every row out

Inside the model a token id does one thing: it selects a row of the embedding table, a vector of a few thousand learned numbers. That vector, and the vectors of every earlier token, are what the layers of attention and feed-forward work on (the subject of the first guide in this series). At the end, the position you are predicting from has one final vector. The LM head multiplies that vector against every row of a second table, one row per vocabulary entry, and the result is one logit per token: 100,000 raw scores.

Hold on to the shape of that output. It is not a word; it is a column of 100,000 numbers, nearly all of them deeply negative, with a handful of tall spikes near the top. Drag the zoom to see how the column looks at full width and how it looks when you sort it and look only at the top.

A synthetic logit column with the statistics of a real one: a few tall entries for the plausible continuations, a long flat tail of everything else. Sorted and zoomed to the top 20, the picture becomes the bar chart every other chapter uses. The tail is not noise to be ignored: at high temperature it is where the odd tokens come from.

05 · TEMPERATURE

Scores become odds, and one number sets how sharp

Logits are not probabilities. Softmax makes them so: raise e to each logit, then divide every result by their sum, so they add to one. Because of the exponential, only the gaps between logits matter: a token 2.3 logits below the leader gets a tenth of its probability, 4.6 below gets a hundredth. The distribution this produces is the model's real output, before anyone picks anything.

pi = ezi / T ÷ Σj ezj / T

Temperature is the T in that formula: every logit is divided by it first. Divide by something small and the gaps widen, so the favourite takes nearly everything. Divide by something large and the gaps shrink toward zero, so every token drifts toward an equal share. At T = 0 the division blows up and, by convention, the top token is simply taken: greedy decoding.

Twelve candidates and their logits, held fixed; only T moves. Watch the entropy readout: it is the honest measure of how much the pick will vary. "Effective choices" is e to the entropy, the number of equally likely faces this die behaves like. Most chat products run between 0.7 and 1.0; code and extraction run at 0 to 0.3.

Temperature is the most misunderstood knob in the API. It does not make the model smarter or more creative; the scores are fixed before it acts. It decides how often the output departs from the single most likely path. Low is safe and repetitive; high is varied and, past about 1.2 with no other filter, increasingly nonsensical, because the hundred-thousand-token tail starts to win rolls.

06 · CUTTING THE TAIL

Three ways to stop the die landing on nonsense

Softmax never gives any token exactly zero. With 100,000 entries, even if each tail token has one chance in a million, the tail as a whole can hold a few percent of the mass, and at temperature 1 you will roll into it every few dozen tokens. One nonsense token derails everything after it. So before the roll, almost every system cuts the tail off and renormalizes what is left. Three rules are in use.

Top-k keeps the k most likely and drops the rest; simple, but blind to how confident the model is. Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to p, so it keeps one token when the model is sure and dozens when it is torn. Min-p keeps every token whose probability is at least some fraction of the leader's, which does the same adapting but holds up better at high temperature. Set them, then roll.

Struck-through rows are cut before the roll; their mass is handed to the survivors (that is the renormalizing). Green is what 200 rolls produced. Start at temperature 1.2 with everything off and notice how often " Lyon" and " not" land. Then switch on min-p 0.1 and roll again. Providers apply these in a fixed order, usually temperature, then top-k, then top-p, then min-p, and most expose only two or three of them.

Practical defaults have converged. For chat, temperature 0.7–1.0 with top-p 0.9–0.95, or min-p 0.05–0.1. For anything a machine will parse, temperature 0 and no sampling knobs at all. Combining top-k 40 and top-p 0.95 is a common belt-and-braces setting; the top-k rarely bites. And note what none of these can do: they filter the model's ranking, they never improve it.

07 · THE WOBBLE

Why "temperature 0" is not quite deterministic

Greedy decoding should be a pure function: same prompt, same model, same top token every time. In practice the same request to the same API at temperature 0 occasionally returns different text. The reason is not randomness in the sampler. It is in the logits.

A GPU computes each logit as a sum of thousands of products in reduced precision, and the order it adds them in depends on how the server batched your request with other people's. Different order, different rounding, different last digits. Almost always harmless, but when two tokens are within a hair of each other the winner flips, and every token after that is conditioned on a different history. Mixture-of-experts routing adds the same effect at every layer. The fix, batch-invariant kernels that add in a fixed order, exists since 2025 and costs some throughput, so most providers do not run it. Add real sampling on top and you get the second kind of variation, the one that is supposed to be there. The box below generates from a tiny model (a trigram model over the toy tokens from chapter 01, so the text is nonsense, but the sampling arithmetic is the real one) three times with the same settings.

Orange marks the first token where a run departs from run 1; everything after it is a different sentence. At temperature 0 all three are identical (this page has no GPU batching to wobble). Push temperature to 1.6 with min-p off and watch raw byte tokens appear: the tail winning rolls. Push repetition penalty up at low temperature and watch the loops break.

What to do about it. If you need reproducibility, fix temperature at 0 and a seed, and still design for the occasional flip: compare outputs semantically, not byte for byte. If you are evaluating a model, run each prompt several times and report the spread; a single run at temperature 0 is one sample, not the truth. And if two runs disagree at a near-tie, that is useful information: the model was genuinely unsure there.

08 · THE OTHER KNOBS

Everything else in the request, and where it acts

The sampling parameters in an API request all act on the same column of logits, in a fixed order, between the LM head and the die. Knowing the order tells you what each can and cannot do. Penalties act first, on raw logits, using the text so far. Then logit bias. Then temperature, then the truncation rules of chapter 06, then the roll. Stop sequences and the token limit watch the output after the roll.

A caution on penalties. They were designed for small models that looped. Modern models rarely loop at sensible temperatures, and a frequency penalty on a 2,000-token code file punishes every repeated variable name and bracket. Default them to zero; reach for them only when you see actual repetition, and prefer a small presence penalty over a frequency one.

09 · STRUCTURED OUTPUT

Valid JSON every time, because the invalid tokens were never allowed

Asking a model nicely for JSON works most of the time. "Most" is the problem: one stray sentence before the brace, one trailing comma, one unescaped quote, and the parser downstream fails. Constrained decoding removes the "most". Before each roll, a grammar compiled from your schema computes which tokens are legal at this exact position, and every other token's probability is set to zero. The die cannot land on an invalid face.

Step through a small schema below. At each position the left column shows what the model wants to say; struck-through rows are what the grammar forbids right now. The output on the right can only ever be a document that matches the schema.

schema: {"name": string, "age": integer, "member": boolean}

Each step: the grammar's state names the legal tokens, the mask zeroes the rest, the renormalized distribution is sampled at low temperature. Notice how often the model's favourite is illegal at the start and legal later: constraint mostly bites at boundaries (opening, between fields, closing), and lets the model speak freely inside a string.

Two caveats that matter in practice. Constraint guarantees shape, not truth: a forced integer can still be the wrong integer, and an over-tight schema can push a model into confident nonsense because its honest answer ("unknown") was not a legal token. And the order of fields in your schema is the order the model must commit to them, so put the fields that need reasoning last and consider a free-text "reasoning" field first. Tool calls in modern APIs are this same mechanism with the tool signature as the grammar.

10 · READING CONFIDENCE

The probabilities are a free signal most people throw away

Every token the model emits came out of a distribution, and most APIs will hand you that distribution's numbers if you ask: the logprob of the chosen token and of the top few alternatives. A logprob is just the natural log of the probability, so 0 is certainty, −0.69 is a coin flip, −2.3 is one in ten, −4.6 is one in a hundred. On a log scale multiplying becomes adding, which is why the probability of a whole answer is the sum of its tokens' logprobs.

Entropy is the same idea applied to the whole distribution at one step rather than to the token that happened to win: how spread out the model's belief was. Perplexity averages the chosen tokens' negative logprobs over a text and exponentiates, giving the effective number of choices per token. The toy trigram model knows only this page's three kilobytes, so its perplexity is far higher than a real model's on anything it has not seen; what transfers is the shape. Hover a token for its logprob. Tokens the toy model has never seen get a floor probability and show as the darkest orange.

Uses, in rough order of value. Classification confidence: ask for one token and read its probability instead of its text. Hallucination triage: a run of low-probability tokens inside a factual claim is a flag worth a second look. Routing: an easy prompt has low entropy throughout and can go to a cheaper model. Evaluation: perplexity on held-out text is how pretraining progress is measured. And speculative decoding from the speed guide is nothing but these probabilities compared between two models.

11 · TRAPS AND THE FRONTIER

Thirty years of the same idea, and the places it leaks

The tokenizer is the oldest part of a modern model's stack, and the seams between it and everything else are where the odd failures live. The best-known seam is the prompt boundary.

Where 2026 stands

Vocabularies have grown to 200k–260k and the token premium for non-English text has roughly halved, but the architecture is unchanged since GPT-2: a frozen byte-level BPE in front of the model. Constrained decoding is now a first-class API feature rather than a library trick. Min-p has joined top-p in most open inference engines. Deterministic inference exists but is paid for in throughput. The live question is whether tokenizer-free models (byte-level patching in BLT, learned chunking in H-Net) reach frontier quality; if they do, chapters 01 to 03 of this page become history and chapters 05 to 10 stay exactly as they are.

12 · WHY IT MATTERS TO ME

What this has to do with the thing you are talking to

Everything you were told about how to use a model, this page can now explain. Why the advice is "temperature 0 for tools, around 0.7 for writing": tool arguments must be the single most likely path, and prose should wander a little. Why structured-output mode fixed your broken JSON: the grammar mask, not a better model. Why a model can write a sonnet and miscount letters: it never saw letters. Why your non-English users cost more and hit context limits sooner: fertility.

Some things to carry into your own work. Budget in tokens, not words, and measure your real fertility on your real inputs: a JSON-heavy agent loop can run at twice the tokens you estimated from the prose. Put the schema on when a parser will read the answer, and put a free-text reasoning field before the fields that need judgement. Use logprobs where a yes/no decision matters; the probability is a better signal than the word. Expect the occasional flip at temperature 0 and compare outputs by meaning. And when a model fails at spelling, counting or arithmetic on long numbers, remember you are watching the tokenizer, not the model, and ask it to spell the thing out.

Finally, the connection to the two earlier guides. The attention guide is what happens between the embedding lookup and the LM head. The speed guide is how fast that loop turns. This page is the two ends of the loop: how text becomes the numbers it works on, and how its numbers become the next piece of text. Together they are the whole of inference.
