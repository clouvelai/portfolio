Downhill: how gradient descent and backpropagation teach a network

Almost every model you have used learned the same way. It made a guess, a single number measured how wrong the guess was, and every one of its parameters was nudged a little in the direction that would have made that number smaller. Two ideas make this work. Backpropagation answers "which way is down?" for billions of parameters at once. Gradient descent decides how far to step and in what manner. This page builds both from a single slope up to a transformer, with a real network training live below.

step 0loss —accuracy —gradient size —parameters —

A real neural network, written from scratch in this page: 2 inputs (a point's x and y), two hidden layers of 8–32 neurons (each neuron is a weighted sum of its inputs passed through the activation), and 1 output squashed by a sigmoid into the probability of orange, scored with cross-entropy averaged over the batch. It is learning to separate blue points from orange ones. The shading is what it currently believes about every location: blue where it would say blue, orange where it would say orange, pale where it is unsure. Each step takes 32 random points, runs them forward, measures the loss, runs backpropagation to get a gradient for every parameter, and lets the optimizer move them. On the spiral, watch the loss (which starts a little above 0.69, because random weights make overconfident guesses) settle near 0.69 for a few hundred steps (0.69 = −ln 0.5, the loss of a model answering 50/50, i.e. a coin flip) and then fall off a cliff as the network discovers how to bend its boundary. The wait is a plateau: until the network finds the winding shape, nudges that help some points hurt others about equally, so the gradient is small and nearly directionless (chapter 02's saddles). Swap to plain gradient descent and it takes far longer. Push the learning rate to the right until the loss turns jagged.

00 · PLAIN WORDS

Every term, in one breath

Read this first or come back to it. Every dotted word on the page links here. No calculus is assumed; where a symbol appears, the plain words come first.

Parameter, weight θ, w
One of the adjustable numbers inside a model. Learning changes these and nothing else. A weight multiplies an input; a bias is a weight that is added instead. The hero network has a few hundred; a frontier model has hundreds of billions, and the largest open ones pass a trillion.The knobs on a mixing desk.
Loss, loss function L
One number that measures how wrong the model's outputs are on some data. Lower is better, zero is perfect. Everything in training exists to push this one number down.The score in a game you are trying to lose.
Cross-entropy, MSE
The two most common losses. Cross-entropy is −log of the probability the model gave to the right answer: −log 1 = 0 (certain and right), −log 0.5 = 0.69 (a coin flip), −log 0.01 = 4.6 (gave the right answer only 1%). Why a log: the probability of a whole sentence is the product of each token's probability, and a log turns that product into a sum, so the loss for a sentence is simply the sum of per-token losses. It also punishes near-zero probabilities without limit. (Log here is the natural log, ln.) It is what language models use. Mean squared error (MSE) averages (prediction − target)² and is used when the answer is a number.Cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes far more than timid ones.
Derivative, slope
How much an output changes per unit change of one input, at a particular point. If nudging a weight up by 0.001 raises the loss by 0.003, the derivative there is about 3. Positive means "increasing this makes the loss worse"; negative means it helps.How steep the road is right where you are standing, and which way it tilts.
Gradient ∇L
The list of all the slopes at once: one partial derivative per parameter. Together they point in the direction that increases the loss fastest. So the opposite direction, −∇L, is the steepest way down. The gradient has exactly as many numbers as the model has parameters.A compass that points uphill. You walk the other way.
Partial derivative ∂L/∂w
The slope with respect to one parameter while every other parameter is held still. Read ∂L/∂w aloud as "how much L changes per unit change in w".Turning one knob while taping the others in place.
Gradient descent
The rule θ ← θ − η·∇L: move every parameter a small step against its slope, recompute, repeat. Each repetition is a step. It finds a low point, not necessarily the lowest one.Walking downhill in fog, one step at a time, checking the slope under your feet before each.
Learning rate η
The multiplier on the gradient: how big each step is. The single most important setting in training. Too small and nothing happens; too big and the steps overshoot the valley and the loss goes up.Stride length.
Loss landscape
The loss imagined as the height of a surface over all possible parameter settings. With two parameters it is a hilly map you can draw; with a billion it is a surface in a billion dimensions that no one can draw, but the same words (valley, ridge, slope) still apply.Terrain. Training is hiking it blindfolded.
Local minimum, saddle point
A local minimum is a spot lower than everything near it but maybe not the lowest anywhere. A saddle point is flat in every direction but curves up in some and down in others, like a mountain pass. In high dimensions saddles are far more common than bad minima, and they slow descent because the slope near them is tiny.A mountain pass: the valley floor if you look one way, the summit if you look the other.
Curvature, Hessian, condition number
How fast the slope itself changes. The Hessian is the table of all second derivatives, N × N entries, far too big to build for a real model. When curvature is steep in one direction and gentle in another (a high condition number), plain gradient descent zigzags across the steep walls and crawls along the gentle floor.A long narrow ravine. Easy to bounce between the walls, hard to make progress along it.
Stochastic gradient descent SGD, mini-batch
Computing the gradient on a small random sample (a mini-batch) instead of all the data. The estimate is noisy but cheap, and you get many more steps for the same compute. Every modern model trains on mini-batches. Careful: "SGD" has two meanings. Here it means the mini-batch idea, which Adam also uses; in an optimizer menu, "SGD" means the plain update rule θ ← θ − η∇L (perhaps with momentum), as opposed to Adam.Polling a hundred voters instead of the whole country. Slightly wrong, enormously faster.
Batch size, gradient noise
How many examples (or tokens) are averaged into one gradient. Larger batches give a less noisy direction, but past a "critical batch size" doubling the batch no longer halves the number of steps you need. Noise shrinks as 1/√batch.More voters per poll: the answer firms up, but slowly.
Chain rule
The calculus fact that makes backprop possible: if a affects b and b affects c, then (how c responds to a) = (how c responds to b) × (how b responds to a). Slopes along a chain multiply. Where a value feeds several places, the contributions add.Exchange rates. Dollars to euros to yen is the product of the two rates.
Computational graph
The model's calculation written as a network of small operations (multiply, add, tanh, softmax), each node knowing only its own inputs and outputs. Frameworks build it automatically as your code runs.A recipe broken into single steps, with arrows showing which bowl goes into which.
Forward pass
Running the graph from inputs to loss, computing every intermediate value. During training those intermediate values are saved, because the backward pass needs them.Taking the exam, keeping your scratch paper.
Backward pass, backpropagation
Running the graph in reverse, from the loss back to every parameter, applying the chain rule at each node. One backward pass produces the full gradient, every parameter's slope, at about twice the cost of the forward pass. It is an efficient way to compute; it is not itself a learning rule.The teacher marking the exam from the final answer backwards, telling every line of working how much it contributed to the mistake.
Automatic differentiation, reverse mode
Computing exact derivatives by applying the chain rule to the real program, operation by operation. Forward mode carries slopes alongside the forward pass and needs one pass per input parameter. Reverse mode (backprop) needs one pass per output, and a loss is one output. That asymmetry is why reverse mode wins: one forward plus one backward costs about three forward passes in total, against about 1.5N for forward mode (a factor of N/2) and N for nudging (a factor of N/3).Asking one question of a billion witnesses, versus asking a billion questions of one witness.
Jacobian, vector-Jacobian product
The Jacobian of an operation is its full table of slopes: every output against every input. Backprop never builds it. Each operation just knows how to take the incoming gradient and return the outgoing one, a "vector-Jacobian product", which is far cheaper.Knowing how to pass a note along without photocopying the whole filing cabinet.
Autograd
The part of PyTorch, JAX and friends that records the graph as your code runs and calls the right backward rule for each operation when you write loss.backward(). You write the forward pass; the backward pass is generated.A clerk who watches you cook and writes the recipe down in reverse.
Activation function, nonlinearity
A simple bend applied after each layer's multiply-and-add: ReLU (zero below 0, unchanged above), tanh and sigmoid (S-shaped squashes), GELU and SwiGLU in transformers. Without it, any stack of layers collapses into one big multiply and can only draw straight lines.A hinge. Straight rods with hinges can bend into any shape; straight rods glued end to end stay straight.
Activations (the values)
Confusingly, also the name for the intermediate numbers a forward pass produces. These are what must be stored for the backward pass, and for a large model they can take more memory than the parameters.The scratch paper from the exam.
Vanishing gradients
When the chain of multiplied slopes through many layers is made of numbers below 1, the product shrinks toward zero and the early layers receive almost no signal. Sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so ten sigmoid layers can shrink the gradient a millionfold.A message whispered down a line of a hundred people.
Exploding gradients
The same chain made of numbers above 1: the product grows exponentially, steps become huge and the loss jumps or becomes NaN. Fixed with careful initialisation, normalisation and gradient clipping.Feedback squeal when the microphone is too close to the speaker.
Initialisation, Xavier, He
How the random starting weights are chosen. Scaled so the size of the signal stays about the same from layer to layer: variance (the spread of the random values, measured as the average squared distance from zero) about 1/fan-in (fan-in: the number of inputs feeding each neuron) for tanh (LeCun; Xavier/Glorot, 2010, averages fan-in and fan-out), 2/fan-in for ReLU (He, 2015). Get it wrong and the network starts out vanishing or exploding.Tuning every amplifier in a chain to unity gain before the concert.
Residual connection, skip connection
Adding each layer's input to its output: x + f(x). The gradient can then flow backward through the "+ x" path untouched, since the slope of x with respect to x is exactly 1. This is what made 100-layer networks trainable (ResNet, 2015) and is the backbone of every transformer.An express lane beside the stop-and-go traffic.
Normalisation, LayerNorm
Rescaling each layer's values to a standard size (mean 0, spread 1) before using them. Keeps signals and gradients in a sane range regardless of depth. Transformers apply LayerNorm or RMSNorm before every block. BatchNorm, the 2015 original, normalises across the examples in a batch instead, which is why it is rare in language models.An automatic volume leveller between every stage of the mixing desk.
Dead ReLU
A ReLU neuron whose input is negative for every example. Its output and its slope are both zero, so no gradient ever reaches its weights and it can never recover. Often caused by a too-large learning rate knocking weights into that region.A light switch that fell off the wall. Nothing you do upstream turns it back on.
Momentum
Instead of stepping along the current gradient, step along a running average of recent gradients. Consistent directions build up speed; back-and-forth zigzags cancel out. Polyak, 1964.A heavy ball rolling downhill instead of a hiker re-checking at every step.
Adam
Published in 2014; in its AdamW form (chapter 07) the default optimizer since about 2018. Keeps two running averages per parameter: of the gradient (momentum) and of the gradient squared (how big it usually is). Steps by the first divided by the square root of the second, so each parameter moves at a sensible rate no matter the scale of its gradient. Costs two extra numbers of memory per parameter.Cruise control fitted separately to each of a billion knobs.
Weight decay, AdamW
A small pull of every weight toward zero each step, which discourages the model from relying on any huge weight. AdamW (2017) applies it separately from Adam's scaling, which turned out to matter. A typical setting for language models is 0.1.A gentle spring tying every knob back toward the middle.
Learning-rate schedule, warmup
Changing η during training. Warmup ramps it up from near zero over the first several thousand steps (Llama 3 405B used 8,000), while Adam's averages are still unreliable; then it decays (cosine, linear, or warmup-stable-decay) so the final steps are small and precise.Ease into a run, cruise, then slow to a walk before you stop.
Gradient norm, clipping
The gradient norm is the length of the whole gradient as one arrow: the square root of the sum of all its squared entries. Clipping caps that length (1.0 is the usual cap for language models): if the arrow is too long, it is shrunk, keeping its direction. Protects against the occasional freak batch.A speed limiter that does not touch the steering.
Gradient accumulation
Running several small mini-batches forward and backward, adding their gradients, and only then taking one step. Gives the effect of a big batch on a GPU that cannot fit it.Polling in four shifts, then deciding once.
Activation checkpointing
Saving only some of the forward pass's intermediate values and recomputing the rest during the backward pass. Trades about a third more compute for a large cut in memory. Not to be confused with saving the model to disk.Keeping only every tenth page of scratch work and redoing the rest when the teacher asks.
Mixed precision, loss scaling
Doing the heavy math in 16-bit numbers for speed while keeping a 32-bit master copy of the weights. In fp16, tiny gradients round to zero, so the loss is multiplied by a large factor before backprop and the gradients divided back afterwards. bf16 has a wider range and usually needs no scaling.Measuring in millimetres so the small numbers don't disappear, then converting back.
Finite differences, gradient check
Estimating a slope by actually nudging a parameter: (L(w + ε) − L(w − ε)) / 2ε. Too slow to train with (two forward passes per parameter), but the standard way to test that a backward pass is correct.Checking the calculator's answer by doing one sum by hand.
Logit, softmax
From the token guide: logits are the raw scores a language model gives every token; softmax turns them into probabilities that sum to 1. The gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logits is just (probabilities − the one-hot answer; "one-hot" means a list of zeros with a single 1 at the correct token), which is where backprop through a language model starts.Raw votes, then percentages.
Overfitting, generalisation
Gradient descent only sees the training data. When training loss keeps falling but loss on held-out data rises, the model is memorising rather than learning a rule that generalises. The held-out set is the only way to tell, and the standard fix is early stopping: keep the checkpoint from the step where held-out loss was lowest.Memorising last year's exam answers.
Second-order methods, preconditioning
Optimizers that use curvature, not just slope, to choose both the direction and the size of each step (Newton's method is the textbook case). The exact Hessian is out of reach, so modern versions (Shampoo, SOAP, Muon) use cheap approximations of its shape per weight matrix.A hiker who can feel how the slope is changing, not just how steep it is, and so knows where the valley floor is likely to be.

01 · THE SLOPE

One parameter, one question: if I nudge it, does the loss go up or down?

Forget networks for a moment. Imagine a model with a single parameter w, and a loss that depends on it. Plot loss against w and you get a curve. Training wants the bottom of the curve, but it cannot see the curve. All it can do is stand at the current value of w and ask one question: if I move w a tiny bit, what happens to the loss?

The answer is the derivative, the slope of the curve at that point. You can estimate it the obvious way: nudge w by a small amount h, measure the loss again, and divide the change by h. As h shrinks, the estimate becomes the exact slope. A positive slope means increasing w makes things worse, so decrease it. A negative slope means increase it. That is the whole of gradient descent in one dimension: w ← w − η × slope, where the learning rate η sets how far to trust the slope.

loss —nudge estimate —exact slope —estimate error —next w —

The orange line joins the loss at w and at w + h: the nudge estimate. The dashed line is the exact tangent. Slide h toward zero and the two lines merge; the error shrinks with h. "Take one step" moves w by −η × slope. The curve is loss = 0.25(w − 0.5)² + 0.7 sin(2.2w) + 1.5, which has two valleys. These η values are large because this toy curve is gentle; a language model's peak η is around 10⁻⁴ (chapter 08), since its landscape is far steeper. From the default w = 1.70, small η settles into the right-hand valley, which is not the lowest one: the valley on the left is deeper, and descent never finds out. At η = 0.6, just above this valley's 2/c limit of about 0.57, the steps overshoot and settle into a bounce between w ≈ 1.70 and 2.10 that never dies out (on a pure bowl it would grow; here the curve's walls bend and cap it); above 1 they leap between valleys erratically (the rule is deterministic, but tiny differences in where you start send it to different places), because the slope is only true right where you stand. Drag w to the far left and the same rule finds the deeper valley.

Notice the nudge method's cost. One extra loss evaluation buys one slope. For one parameter that is nothing. For a model with seven billion parameters it is seven billion extra forward passes per step, and chapter 04 shows how absurd that is. Backpropagation exists to get every slope for the price of about two extra forward passes (three in all, counting the forward pass you were running anyway). But first, what does "slope" mean when there are many parameters?

02 · DOWNHILL IN MANY DIRECTIONS

The gradient: every slope at once, pointing uphill

With two parameters the loss is a surface over a flat map, a landscape, and you can draw it as a contour map: lines joining points of equal loss, like a hiking map. At any point there is one slope per parameter, each measured with the others held still. Those are the partial derivatives, and stacked together they form the gradient, an arrow pointing in the direction where the loss climbs fastest. Its negative points straight downhill, at right angles to the contour lines. Gradient descent steps against the gradient, along that negative arrow. A real model does exactly this, except the map has billions of directions instead of two.

Two things go wrong even on a map you can see. The first is the learning rate. On a bowl, the steps shrink by themselves as the slope flattens near the bottom, but if η is too large each step overshoots the valley and lands higher on the opposite wall, and the next overshoots more. The second is shape. When the valley is a long narrow ravine, the gradient points mostly across it, at the steep walls, rather than along the gentle floor where the minimum is. Gradient descent zigzags. The condition number, steepest curvature divided by gentlest, measures how bad that is: a condition number of 40 means the safe step for the steep direction is 40 times too small for the gentle one. In deep networks it runs into the thousands or more. Chapter 07's optimizers exist largely to fix this.

There is a precise rule behind both. On a bowl whose curvature (how fast the slope changes) is c, a step of size η converges only if η < 2/c. At η = 1/c it lands on the bottom in one step; between 1/c and 2/c it overshoots but each bounce is smaller; above 2/c each bounce is bigger and it diverges. With several directions, the steepest one sets the limit for all of them, which is exactly the ravine problem. The ravine below has curvature 10 across and 0.25 along, so the limit is η < 0.2, and at that η the gentle direction creeps forward 0.25 × 0.2 = 5% of the remaining distance per step.

Click anywhere on the map to start from there. The orange arrow is −∇L, scaled by η: where the next step will go. The ringed cross marks the minimum. On the ravine, run at the default η: the path drops into the floor and then crawls. Raise η toward 0.2 and it starts zigzagging across the walls; past 0.2 it flies off, because the steep direction overshoots on every step even though the gentle direction would tolerate a step forty times larger (the curvatures are 10 and 0.25). On two valleys, starting points on the right settle in the shallower valley. On the saddle, start on the horizontal midline and the descent stalls at the centre, where the slope is almost zero in every direction, before escaping slowly.

A reassurance about high dimensions. The picture of getting stuck in a bad local minimum mostly comes from two-dimensional drawings like these. In a space of a billion directions, a point that curves upward in every single direction is rare; far more common are saddle points, which curve up in some directions and down in others, and plateaus. Descent slows down near them but does not get trapped forever, especially with the noise of chapter 06. Large networks also have many good minima of nearly equal loss. The two-valley map above is a deliberately unfair 2-D toy; in a billion dimensions the evidence (from many training runs that land at near-identical loss from different random starts) is that the practical enemies are slowness and instability, not bad valleys.

03 · THE CHAIN RULE

How a slope travels through a calculation

A network is not one function; it is hundreds of small ones in a row. Multiply by a weight, add a bias, apply tanh, compare with the target, square. Write it as a computational graph, one node per small operation, and the question "how does the loss respond to this weight?" becomes a question about a path through the graph. The chain rule answers it: the slope along a path is the product of the slopes of each link. If a weight's change gets doubled by one node and then halved by the next, the loss responds to it at 1×.

The trick that makes this efficient is to start at the end. The loss's slope with respect to itself is 1. Each node receives the slope of the loss with respect to its own output (call it the upstream gradient), multiplies it by its own local slope, and passes the result to its inputs. Each node only needs to know its own tiny derivative, which is simple: an add node has slope 1 to each input, a multiply node has slope equal to the other input, tanh has slope 1 − tanh², subtracting a fixed target has slope 1, and squaring d has slope 2d. The branch rule matters more than it looks. In a transformer every token's representation is used by many later tokens through attention, and every weight matrix is used at every position. Each use sends back its own share of blame, and the shares add. That is why one weight's gradient in a language model is a sum over every token in the batch.

04 · BACKPROPAGATION

Why one backward pass beats a billion nudges

Chapter 03 did the chain rule on five operations. Backpropagation is that procedure applied to the whole network, organised so nothing is computed twice. Run the forward pass and keep every intermediate value. Start the backward pass at the loss with gradient 1. Visit the nodes in exactly reverse order; at each, multiply the arriving gradient by the local slope and hand it to the node's inputs, adding where branches meet. When the sweep reaches the start, every parameter holds its partial derivative. One forward, one backward, all N slopes.

Why backwards? Because the loss is a single number and the parameters are billions. Going backwards from that single output, one sweep collects the slope to every input. Going forwards, which is also a valid way to apply the chain rule (forward-mode differentiation), each sweep carries the effect of one input to the output, so you need one sweep per parameter. The nudge method from chapter 01 also needs one full forward pass per parameter: a little cheaper per sweep than forward mode, but only approximate. The instrument shows what each costs for a single training step.

Time for one gradient, by method. Forward pass ≈ 2 operations per parameter per token. Nudging (one-sided, as in chapter 01): one extra forward pass per parameter; the two-sided check of chapter 10 doubles that. Forward-mode: one forward-sized sweep per parameter (about 1.5× a forward pass each). Reverse mode (backprop): one forward plus one backward ≈ 3× a forward pass in total, the 6·N·D rule from the training guide (training compute ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens). Bar lengths are on a log scale.

Two consequences show up everywhere. First, memory: training must keep every layer's saved activations until the backward pass reaches that layer, so the memory bill grows with depth, sequence length and batch size, on top of the 16 bytes per parameter for weights, gradients and Adam state. Second, you never write any of this by hand. Autograd records the graph as your forward code runs and replays it backwards when you call loss.backward(); each operation ships with its own backward rule, a vector-Jacobian product that takes the incoming gradient and returns the outgoing one without ever building the full table of slopes. The hero network at the top of this page does the same thing by hand in about twenty lines of this page's own script (view source to read it).

05 · DEEP NETWORKS

Multiplying many slopes: vanishing, exploding, and the fix

The chain rule multiplies. In a deep network the gradient reaching layer 1 is the product of one factor per layer above it: each layer's weights times its activation's slope. If those factors are typically below 1, the product shrinks exponentially with depth and the early layers receive almost nothing: vanishing gradients. If they are typically above 1, the product grows exponentially: exploding gradients. For twenty years this was the reason deep networks did not train. Sigmoid, the textbook activation, has a slope of at most 0.25, so every sigmoid layer cuts the gradient by at least a factor of four.

Four fixes, all still in use, turned depth from a liability into the whole point. Better activations: ReLU's slope is exactly 1 wherever it is active. Better initialisation: start the weights at the scale that keeps the signal's size constant from layer to layer. And residual connections: compute x + f(x) instead of f(x), so the backward pass always has a path whose slope is exactly 1, no matter what f does. Each block's slope is 1 + (f's slope), so the gradient no longer shrinks layer after layer (f's slope would have to cancel the 1 almost exactly); it can still grow, since a product of many numbers a little above 1 compounds, which is why residual branches are kept small (below) and clipping exists. And normalisation layers, which rescale each layer's output back to a standard size, so the forward signal cannot drift. (Exploding gradients have one more, last-resort guard, clipping, in chapter 08.) The instrument runs a real 30-layer network with real backprop; nothing here is a sketch.

Each bar is the size of the gradient arriving at one layer's weights, from layer 1 (nearest the input, left) to the last layer (right), averaged over 8 random inputs. The scale is logarithmic: every gridline is a factor of 1,000. Init scale 1.00× is the textbook choice (Xavier for sigmoid and tanh, He for ReLU). Sigmoid at default: the first layer gets a gradient around a billion billion times smaller than the last, so it effectively never learns. Switch to ReLU and the bars level out. Then push init scale to 1.5× and every gradient explodes about 100,000-fold (from about 3 to about 3 × 10⁵), evenly: the forward signal grows 1.5× per layer going up while the backward signal grows 1.5× per layer coming down, so layer 1 against the last layer still reads "about equal". Then tick residual connections and see every case flatten. (The residual branches here are scaled by 1/√depth, the same trick GPT-2 used at initialisation: each residual branch adds a bit of variance (spread) to the stream, so with L branches the variance grows like L, and shrinking each branch by 1/√L keeps it bounded.)

One more failure lives here: the dead ReLU. A ReLU passes nothing and has slope zero whenever its input is negative. If a big update pushes a neuron's bias so far down that its input is negative for every example, it outputs zero forever and receives zero gradient forever. Too-high learning rates can kill a large fraction of a network this way, silently. Smooth relatives like GELU and SwiGLU, used in transformers, keep a small slope below zero partly for this reason.

06 · NOISY STEPS

Stochastic gradient descent: a rough direction, many more times

The true gradient is the average over all the training data, and for a language model that would mean all of its trillions of training tokens for every single step. Nobody computes it. Stochastic gradient descent computes the gradient on a random mini-batch instead. Each mini-batch gives a slightly different arrow, scattered around the true one, and the scatter shrinks as the square root of the batch size: four times the batch halves the noise. The bargain is that a noisy step is so much cheaper than an exact one that you can take thousands of noisy steps for the price of one exact step, and because each mini-batch's estimate is unbiased (right on average), its errors point in random directions and mostly cancel over many steps. Not completely: with a fixed learning rate the parameters keep jittering around the minimum, which is one reason chapter 08 shrinks η at the end. The batch-size trade-off has a limit, the critical batch size: below it, noise is the bottleneck and doubling the batch halves the steps you need; above it, the step direction is already accurate, and what limits progress is how far each step can safely go, the η < 2/c limit of chapter 02. A bigger batch only sharpens a direction that is already sharp enough, so it burns compute without saving steps.

The noise is not only tolerated; it helps. It shakes the parameters off saddle points and plateaus, and it seems to steer training toward broad, flat valleys rather than narrow ones, which tend to generalise better to unseen data. The thin orange arrows are 30 steps that 30 different random mini-batches would take from the same point. At batch size 1 they spray in all directions; at 400 they collapse onto the blue arrow. Press "run SGD" to walk 150 noisy steps: small batches wander but still get there, and keep jittering around the minimum, never quite settling, unless the learning rate is lowered. That jitter is why every real schedule decays the learning rate at the end (chapter 08). "Examples read" counts the cost.

When a batch will not fit in GPU memory, gradient accumulation fakes it: run several small batches forward and backward, add their gradients without stepping, and take one step at the end. The arithmetic is identical to one big batch, provided each micro-batch's loss is divided by the number of micro-batches (otherwise the step is k times too big) and no layer mixes examples within a batch (BatchNorm does: it normalises with the statistics of whatever batch it sees, so a quarter-size micro-batch gets different statistics and different gradients; LayerNorm works on each example alone, so it is safe). It is how a fine-tune on a single GPU gets an effective batch of 64 sequences while only fitting 4 at a time.

07 · BETTER THAN DOWNHILL

Momentum and Adam: using the gradient's history

Plain descent forgets everything after each step. Two cheap memories fix most of its problems. Momentum keeps a running average of recent gradients and steps along that. In a ravine, the across-the-walls components flip sign every step and cancel in the average, while the along-the-floor component points the same way every step and accumulates. The zigzag damps and the crawl speeds up. It is a heavy ball instead of a nervous hiker.

Adam adds a second memory per parameter: a running average of the squared gradient, which measures how big that parameter's gradient usually is. Each step divides the momentum by the square root of that, so a parameter with a huge, noisy gradient takes cautious steps and a parameter with a tiny, consistent gradient takes full-sized ones. When a parameter's gradient is consistent, m/√v is close to ±1, so it moves at roughly η per step whatever the scale of its own gradient; when the gradient is noisy, m averages towards zero while √v stays large, and the step shrinks. (Because m and v start at zero, both are biased towards zero for the first steps, and not equally: at step 1, m holds 10% of the gradient but √v holds only about 3% of its size, so the step would be about 3× too large. Adam divides m by 1 − β₁ᵗ and v by 1 − β₂ᵗ, a "bias correction" that fades out after a few thousand steps.) This is a crude version of knowing the curvature, without ever computing it, and it is why Adam works with almost no tuning on networks whose different parts have gradients differing by factors of a thousand. Every large language model covered in this series of field guides was trained with Adam or AdamW; the first frontier exception, Kimi K2 (2025), used Muon for its weight matrices (chapter 11). RMSprop, in the race below, is Adam without the momentum: it divides the raw gradient by √v.

Four optimizers start from the same point and take 300 steps each, every one at a learning rate tuned for this landscape; the multiplier scales them all together. Click the map to move the start. On the curved valley, plain descent stalls on the long bend while Adam rides it to the minimum at the ringed cross. On the saddle, the start is almost exactly on the ridge where the slope is tiny: plain descent waits for the slope to grow; Adam, which divides by the gradient's size, walks off straight away. Turn the multiplier up and plain descent is the first to blow up (on the ravine from about 1.3×): its step is η times the steep wall's slope, while RMSprop and Adam divide that slope back out.

The last refinement, AdamW, is a bug fix that turned into a standard. Weight decay, a gentle pull of every weight toward zero, used to be added into the gradient, where Adam's division then weakened it for exactly the weights with large gradients. Loshchilov and Hutter applied it as a separate step instead, and models generalised better. Newer optimizers go further toward using curvature: Shampoo and SOAP approximate it per weight matrix, and Muon, which replaces each matrix's update with its nearest orthogonal matrix (mathematically, Shampoo's per-matrix rescaling computed from the current update alone), trained Kimi K2's trillion parameters in 2025. Chapter 11 has the family tree.

08 · STEERING THE RUN

Schedules, warmup and clipping: the controls on a real run

The learning rate is never constant in a real run. It follows a schedule. It starts near zero and ramps up over the first several thousand steps (warmup), because at the start the weights are random, the gradients are large and strange, and Adam's running averages have seen almost nothing, so a full-size step would be a step in a badly measured direction. Bias correction (chapter 07) fixes the averages' size, not their quality: an average of a dozen strange gradients is still a poor guide. Then it holds or slowly falls. At the end it decays toward zero, because chapter 06's jitter around the minimum only stops when the steps get small. Cosine decay was the standard for years; warmup-stable-decay (WSD) holds the rate flat and decays only in the final stretch, which lets a lab extend a run without restarting it.

The second control is gradient clipping. Every step, compute the gradient's total length, the norm. If it exceeds a threshold (1.0 for almost every language model), shrink the whole gradient to that length, keeping its direction. Ordinary steps are untouched; the occasional freak batch that would have produced a giant step is tamed. Early in training, when gradients are large, clipping can fire on a noticeable share of steps (the trace below clips about one step in ten); later it should be rare. A gradient-norm chart is one of the first things an engineer looks at when a run misbehaves: spikes there come before spikes in the loss.

09 · INSIDE A TRANSFORMER

Where backprop starts in a language model, and where it goes

Every chapter so far applies unchanged to a transformer; it just has more nodes. The backward pass starts at the output. The model produced logits for every token in the vocabulary, softmax turned them into probabilities p, and the loss is −log p of the token that actually came next. The gradient of that loss with respect to the logits has a remarkably clean form: p − y, where y is 1 for the correct token and 0 for every other. The correct token's logit is pushed up by (1 − its probability); every wrong token's logit is pushed down by exactly its own probability. The wrong token the model was most confident in gets the biggest push down, and the correct token gets the biggest push up when the model gave it little probability. Already right and certain? Almost no gradient at all.

From there the gradient flows down through the stack of blocks along the residual stream, the "x +" path from chapter 05, copied unchanged at every addition and also sent into each block's two branches: attention (where each token looks back at earlier tokens, using a query, key and value vector computed from its representation; the attention guide covers it) and the feed-forward network (a small two-layer network applied to each token separately). Through attention it splits three ways, into the query, key and value projections, and travels back to every earlier position the token attended to. That is how a mistake on token 500 can adjust how token 20 was represented. It ends at the embedding table (the lookup that turns each token's id into its starting vector), where only the rows for tokens that appeared in the batch receive any gradient (unless the model ties its input and output embeddings, in which case the output end touches every row).

Two engineering facts make this pass affordable at scale. Saved activations grow with sequence length, layers and batch, and for long contexts they dwarf the weights, so frameworks use activation checkpointing: keep only each block's input, and recompute the block's insides during the backward pass. That costs about one extra forward pass, roughly a third more compute, for a large cut in memory. And the math runs in 16-bit numbers, where, in fp16, gradients lose precision below about 6 × 10⁻⁵ and round to zero near 6 × 10⁻⁸, its smallest number; the cure was loss scaling, multiplying the loss by a large factor before backprop and dividing the gradients after. bf16 keeps fp32's range, which is a big reason it replaced fp16 for training.

10 · WHEN GRADIENTS LIE

Checking the backward pass, and the ways it breaks

A wrong backward pass is the most dangerous bug in machine learning, because it rarely crashes. The loss still falls, a little, more slowly than it should, and the model ends up worse than it could have been, and nothing tells you. The standard defence is the gradient check: for each parameter, nudge it up by ε and down by ε, measure the loss both times, and compare the slope you measured with the slope backprop computed. Chapter 01's slow method, used as a referee. If the relative difference is tiny (around 10⁻⁷ or smaller, i.e. they agree to about seven significant figures), the backward pass is right; a real bug usually shows up far above 10⁻⁴, often at 10⁻² or more.

Below is a small network with 13 parameters learning XOR (output 1 when exactly one of two inputs is 1, a classic problem no straight line can separate). Its backward pass is written by hand. Plant one of four classic bugs and see which parameters the check flags: activation slope (leaving out tanh's local slope, 1 − tanh², as if it were 1), averaging (forgetting to divide the biases' summed gradient by the batch size of 4, so the bias gradients are 4× too big while the weights are right), sign (flipping the error at the output, so every weight climbs uphill), and stale activation (reading the wrong saved value, the hidden layer's input to tanh instead of its output, when computing the output weights' gradient). You will rarely write a backward pass yourself, but these are exactly the mistakes inside custom CUDA kernels, custom autograd functions and hand-fused layers, which is where frontier labs do write them.

"backprop" is what the hand-written backward pass returns; "nudge" is (L(θ + ε) − L(θ − ε)) / 2ε with ε = 10⁻⁵. Relative error = |a − b| / (|a| + |b|). Under 10⁻⁷ is a pass; above 10⁻⁴ is almost certainly a bug; in between, look closer (a kink such as ReLU at exactly 0 can land there). This check runs in 64-bit floats; in 32-bit, round-off alone can reach 10⁻³, so real gradient checks always switch to 64-bit first. Note the nudge is two-sided here (±ε, two forward passes per parameter), which is more accurate than chapter 01's one-sided w + h. Notice that each bug damages a specific set of rows. The general rule: a mistake in the formula for one set of weight gradients flags just those parameters (stale: the three output weights; averaging: the four biases), while a mistake in the error signal passed backward flags every parameter it flows into, which is everything below that point (activation slope: layer 1's nine weights and biases; sign, at the very top: all 13). The pattern of failures points at the line of code.

11 · HISTORY AND FRONTIER

From Cauchy's astronomy to Muon

Gradient descent is older than computers: Cauchy proposed it in 1847 to solve the equations of planetary orbits. Backpropagation was discovered several times over before anyone noticed it could train networks. Most of what you just learned predates the transformer; the transformer exists because these tools made deep networks trainable at all.

12 · WHY IT MATTERS TO ME

Reading a loss curve like an engineer

You will probably never write a backward pass. You will fine-tune models, read training logs and make calls on other people's runs, and every one of those is a conversation about this page. The loss curve is the patient's chart. Pick a symptom.

Some things to carry into your own work. The learning rate is the first knob and usually the only one that matters: for full fine-tuning (continuing to train every weight of a finished model on your own data) start near 1–2 × 10⁻⁵, for LoRA near 1–2 × 10⁻⁴. LoRA freezes the original weights and trains small add-on matrices beside them, a fraction of a percent of the parameters, and one matrix in each pair starts at zero, so training begins from exactly the original model's behaviour. Those few new parameters must carry the whole change, so they need bigger steps. Use warmup and decay even on short runs. Keep clipping at 1.0. If memory runs out on a fine-tune with a long sequence or big batch, the culprit is usually the saved activations of chapter 04 (they grow with batch × sequence length, while weights and optimizer state are fixed), so shorten sequences, use gradient accumulation or turn on checkpointing before you shrink the model. Hold out data before you start and judge by it, never by training loss. And when a model's behaviour surprises you, remember that gradient descent only ever followed the loss it was given: it finds whatever lowers that number, intended or not. Reward hacking, from the training guide, is this same fact at the scale of a whole model.

Where this sits in the series. Attention is what the forward pass computes. Tokens per second is what the forward pass costs. The next token is what goes in and comes out. The training run is the schedule of losses a model is pushed through. This page is the push. Next: evaluation, the discipline of measuring what all this produced, and context engineering for agents.
